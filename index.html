1

Isaiah 43:1–2

But now thus says the LORD, he who created you, O Jacob, he who formed you, O Israel: Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by name; you are mine. When you pass through the waters, I will be with you, and through the rivers, they shall not overwhelm you; when you walk through fire you shall not be burned, and the flame shall not consume you.

❀ ❀ ❀

Look at the tense. I have redeemed you. It is already done. It was done before this dream started. And He does not promise you will miss the river. He promises it will not close over your head. Mary Magdalene was one of the women who travelled with Jesus and paid for the work out of her own money, and He had freed her from seven demons. In John 20 she stands crying outside His empty tomb, so upset that she takes Him for the gardener. He does not argue with her. He says one word. Mary. And she knows Him. That is what I have called you by name looks like in real life. Not an idea. A voice saying your name when you cannot recognise anything else. The night does not know your name. He does.

Notice what Mary did after she heard her name. She did not stay at the tomb. She ran and told the others, and the Church calls her the apostle to the apostles. Being named was not the end of her story. It was the start of her work. You were named at your baptism, in front of the whole Church, and that name is written somewhere no dream can reach. Tonight, if you wake afraid, say your own name to Him, and then say His. Lord, you know me. I am yours.

2

1 John 3:8

Everyone who commits sin is a child of the devil; for the devil has been sinning from the beginning. The Son of God was revealed for this purpose, to destroy the works of the devil.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Greek word is lysē. It means to untie. Not a fort to be stormed. A knot to be undone. Luke uses the same word about a woman. In Luke 13 she had been bent over for eighteen years and could not straighten up, and nobody in the synagogue had named the problem out loud. Jesus said she must be loosed from what held her, said plainly who had tied it and for how long, and called her a daughter of Abraham. He did not tell her off for failing to straighten herself. He called her over and put His hands on her. Whatever is tied around you in the dark was tied by someone whose hands He has already emptied.

Think about what eighteen years does to a person. She would have stopped expecting anything. People would have stopped noticing her. She probably came to the synagogue that day the way she always came, looking at the floor. She did not even ask to be healed. He saw her first and called her over. Some of what weighs on you has been there so long it feels like part of your shape. It is not. It is a knot, and He is the one who unties. You do not have to find the end of the rope. You only have to come when He calls.

3

Luke 10:19

See, I have given you authority to tread on snakes and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy; and nothing will hurt you.

❀ ❀ ❀

Authority is not the same as strength. The Greek is exousia. It means the right to act in someone else’s name. It is given to you. You do not earn it. A few chapters earlier, in Luke 8, we are told who was travelling with Jesus: Mary Magdalene, Joanna, who was married to Herod’s household manager, Susanna, and many other women, paying for the whole mission themselves. They were not helpers at the edge. They were the people this was said to. A dream can frighten you. It cannot cancel your standing, any more than a bad night can cancel your citizenship. And look at the verb. Tread. Not fight. Not win. Just walk over it on your way somewhere else.

Those women did not stop being afraid when they started following Jesus. They watched Him arrested. They watched Him die. And still the authority He gave was theirs the whole time, through the worst week of their lives. Your standing does not go up and down with how you feel. It does not drop at night and come back in the morning. It is not a mood. It is a gift with your name on it. When the dark tells you that you have no right to be at peace, answer it plainly: this was given to me, and it is not mine to lose.

4

Romans 16:20

The God of peace will shortly crush Satan under your feet. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you.

❀ ❀ ❀

Read the twenty verses before this one. Romans 16 greets about ten women by name, several of them called fellow workers and leaders. Phoebe, a deacon, who almost certainly carried this letter to Rome in her own hands. Prisca. Mary, who worked hard. Junia, well known among the apostles. Tryphaena and Tryphosa. Persis. The mother of Rufus. Julia. The sister of Nereus. Paul lists them, and then says God will crush Satan under your feet. Whose feet did he have in mind? Notice too that he calls Him the God of peace. The crushing is how peace comes. It is not the opposite of peace. Gregory of Nyssa taught that evil has no real substance of its own [A]. What goes under your heel is a nothing pretending to be a something [C].

These women are mostly forgotten now. We have a name and a line each. Mary, who worked hard. Persis, who worked hard in the Lord. That is all history kept of them. And Paul, writing to the capital of the world, made sure they were greeted by name before he made his promise. God does not need you to be famous or impressive to put Satan under your feet. He needs you to be His. A line in a letter was enough for Persis. Your name in His book is enough for you. Say it slowly tonight: the God of peace will crush him, soon.

5

Ephesians 6:16

With all of these, take the shield of faith, with which you will be able to quench all the flaming arrows of the evil one.

❀ ❀ ❀

The arrows are already burning when they reach you. The shield does not turn them away. It puts them out. That is a different promise, and a more honest one. You may still feel the hit. Faith takes the fire out of it so nothing spreads and burns all night. A Roman shield was wood covered in leather, and soldiers soaked it in water before a battle. A dry shield burned, and so did the man behind it. Yours was soaked at your baptism. In the Syriac tradition that font is called a womb and the Church a mother: Ephrem sings of it in the Hymns on the Nativity and Jacob of Serugh says the baptised are born there a second time [A]. You were carried once. You were carried again.

There is something tender in the Syriac picture. A mother does not carry a child because the child has earned it. She carries it because it is hers. The Church carried you to the font before you could walk or speak, and the water did its work without asking how strong you were. Women know the weight and the cost of carrying. You may be carrying a great deal right now, for others as well as for yourself. Let yourself be the one who is carried for a moment. The shield was soaked long before tonight’s arrows were lit. It is still wet.

6

James 4:7

Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you.

❀ ❀ ❀

The order matters. Submit first, then resist. Resisting first is only willpower, and willpower is gone by three in the morning. One woman does this perfectly. In Mark 7 a Gentile woman from the coast near Tyre comes about a daughter tormented by a demon. Jesus gives her a hard answer about dogs and children. She does not argue for her dignity. She takes the low place and stays there: yes, Lord, and even the dogs get the crumbs. That is her resistance, and she is the only person in the Gospels who wins an exchange with Him. For that word, go, the demon has left your daughter. She went home and found the child lying quietly. Submitting is not losing. It moves the fight onto ground where you do not have to stand.

Notice that she was a mother praying for her daughter, and she would not leave. Many women know this kind of prayer, the kind you pray for someone else when you cannot fix their suffering. She had no standing, no religious credentials, the wrong nationality, and every reason to be sent away. She stayed anyway and kept the conversation pointed at Him. If you are praying tonight for someone else as well as yourself, take her as your model. You are allowed to keep asking. You are allowed to take the lowest place and refuse to leave it.

7

Matthew 4:10

Jesus said to him, "Away with you, Satan! for it is written, 'Worship the Lord your God, and serve only him.'"

❀ ❀ ❀

Jesus does not argue with the tempter. Three times He simply quotes Scripture, and the third time He sends him away. Maximus the Confessor says this is Adam’s conversation in reverse: Adam negotiated, Christ refused to talk at all [A]. Mary of Egypt lived this. Her life, written by Sophronius of Jerusalem and read in church every Lent, says she spent her youth badly in Alexandria and then lived forty-seven years alone beyond the Jordan. For the first seventeen her own memories attacked her: songs she had sung, food she had eaten, the life she had left. She did not analyse them. She lay on the ground and said the words she knew and waited for the light. You owe your dream no reply beyond the verse. Say it, then stop.

The part of Mary’s story that is easy to miss is how plain her weapons were. She had no books and no teacher. She had a few words and her body on the ground. And Zosimas, the monk who found her at the end of her life, was amazed that she could quote Scripture she had never been taught to read [A]. What you repeat becomes part of you. The verses you say tonight, half asleep, without feeling anything, are not wasted. They are going in. One day they will come out of you when you need them, the way they came out of her in the desert.

8

Psalm 91:11

For he will command his angels concerning you to guard you in all your ways.

❀ ❀ ❀

He commands them. They are not volunteers, and they do not sleep. The Syriac Fathers call angels ʿirē, the Wakeful Ones, a name taken from Daniel’s vision [A]. And the first angel in the Bible is sent to a woman. Hagar, in Genesis 16, was an Egyptian slave in Abraham’s house, pregnant by him, badly treated, running into the desert with nowhere to go. An angel found her at a spring and asked where she had come from and where she was going. She answered by giving God a name: El-Roi, the God who sees me. She is the first person in all of Scripture to name Him. Your sleep is not a gap in the guard. It is the one part of your life you were never asked to watch over.

Hagar was not looking for God when He found her. She was running away. She was angry and hurt and had every right to be. The angel did not scold her for running. He asked two honest questions and then gave her a promise for her child. God is not offended by the state you are in when He finds you. You do not have to tidy yourself up before you are guarded. The Wakeful Ones are sent to you as you are tonight, frightened and tired. And the God who saw Hagar at that spring sees you now.

9

2 Thessalonians 3:3

But the Lord is faithful; he will strengthen you and guard you from the evil one.

❀ ❀ ❀

Paul sets God’s faithfulness against your fear, not against your failure. Two verbs: strengthen and guard, one inside and one outside. Now notice what is missing. There is no condition. He does not say if you are strong enough, or if you slept well, or if you prayed properly. In Luke 2 we meet Anna, a prophet of the tribe of Asher, widowed after seven years of marriage and eighty-four years old. Luke says she had not left the temple since, and worshipped there with fasting and prayer night and day. He records her age and her father’s name as if her endurance were public record. Then she saw what she had waited for. The faithfulness here is His. That is why it holds on the nights you have none.

Anna lost her husband after only seven years. Then she spent the rest of her life praying, night and day, in the same building. People who passed her every day probably thought of her as a sad old widow. Luke calls her a prophet. Her long nights of prayer were not wasted time; they were preparation, and she was in exactly the right place when the child was carried in. Your nights, even the frightening ones, are being counted. God does not waste a single hour you give Him in the dark.

10

Zechariah 3:2

The LORD said to Satan, "The LORD rebuke you, O Satan! The LORD who has chosen Jerusalem rebuke you! Is not this man a brand plucked from the fire?"

❀ ❀ ❀

Joshua the priest stands there in dirty clothes and cannot defend himself. So God does not make him try. The Lord speaks to the accuser directly, and the accused man never says a word in the whole scene. Susanna knew that silence. Her story, in Daniel 13 in the Peshitta and the Greek, tells of a married woman in Babylon trapped alone in her own garden by two elders who were also judges. When she refused them they accused her in public of the very thing they had tried to do, and she was condemned on their word against hers, until Daniel intervened. She did not win the argument. She cried out to God. Burn marks on a stick pulled from a fire prove rescue, not guilt.

Susanna’s accusers were powerful men with good reputations, and the whole community believed them. Her prayer is worth learning. She told God that He knows what is secret and is aware of all things before they happen [A]. She did not ask anyone else to believe her. She asked God to know. Sometimes what hurts most at night is the feeling that nobody sees the truth about you. He does. He knows exactly what is false in what is being said to you, or in what you are saying to yourself. You can leave your defence with Him and rest.

11

Jude 1:9

But when the archangel Michael contended with the devil and disputed about the body of Moses, he did not dare to bring a condemnation of slander against him, but said, "The Lord rebuke you!"

❀ ❀ ❀

Even the archangel will not argue. He hands the matter upward. This is the rule of real spiritual combat: whoever answers in her own name has already stepped onto the enemy’s ground. Amma Sarah was a desert mother in Egypt in the fourth century, one of the women monks came to for counsel; nine of her sayings survive. She fought one temptation for thirteen years and never once debated with it. At the end the thing appeared and said, Sarah, you have beaten me. She answered only: it is not I who overcome you, but my Master, Christ [A]. Thirteen years, and one short sentence. Say the LORD rebuke you and then stop. Anything longer turns into a conversation, and that is what he came for.

Some visitors once came to Amma Sarah, perhaps to humble her, and warned her not to grow proud that monks travelled to see a woman. She answered that she was a woman by nature, but not in her thoughts [A]. She meant that her strength did not come from what other people expected of women. It came from God. You may meet people who think you are fragile because you are afraid at night. Fear is not the same as weakness. Sarah fought thirteen years and won with one sentence that pointed away from herself. You can do the same.

12

Matthew 16:23

But he turned and said to Peter, "Get behind me, Satan! You are a stumbling block to me; for you are setting your mind not on divine things but on human things."

❀ ❀ ❀

Jesus names Satan in the mouth of a friend who meant well. This is the hardest thing to see: the thought that comes dressed as concern for you. Martha of Bethany, sister of Mary and Lazarus, was the one who kept the household running, and she is the one who makes the great confession of faith in John 11. She was not wrong that there was work to do, and not wrong that she was doing it alone. He still told her, gently, that she was worried about many things. Being right is not the same as being on the right side of a thought. So when advice comes about these nights, from people who love you, test it by where it points. Divine things or human things. Not by how kindly it is said.

Martha was a woman who carried a household, and that kind of carrying does not stop when the lights go off. It comes to bed with you: the list, the worry, the feeling that if you stop, things will fall apart. Jesus did not tell Martha her work was worthless. He told her that one thing mattered more, and that the worry was not that thing. When you lie awake running through everything that could go wrong, try to put it down. Lord, I cannot hold all of this. You hold it, and let me rest.

13

Psalm 68:1

Let God rise up, let his enemies be scattered; let those who hate him flee before him.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Church sings this at Easter, and women got there first. Very early, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene and the others walked to the tomb with spices. The Orthodox Church calls them the myrrhbearers and keeps a Sunday in their honour. They were going to do the heavy, ordinary work women have always done for the dead. They expected a body, not a resurrection. And the stone was already rolled away. Now look at the grammar of this verse. Nothing at all is asked of the enemy. Only of God. Let God rise, and the scattering follows by itself. You are not being asked to scatter anything. You are being asked to want Him to stand up. The women who first learned He had were women who walked into the dark anyway.

Think of the courage of those women. Soldiers were guarding the tomb. The disciples were in hiding. Every sensible person would have stayed indoors until it was safe. They went out before sunrise anyway, because love had work to do. The Church remembers them every year on the Sunday of the Myrrhbearers. You are walking through your own dark hours right now. Like them, you may find that the thing you were dreading has already been dealt with by the time you get there. Let God rise. That is your whole prayer tonight.

14

Isaiah 54:17 ○

No weapon that is fashioned against you shall prevail, and you shall confute every tongue that rises against you in judgment. This is the heritage of the servants of the LORD and their vindication from me, says the LORD.

❀ ❀ ❀

Read the chapter this verse closes. It begins sing, o barren one. All through Isaiah 54 Jerusalem is spoken to as a childless, abandoned wife: no children, no standing, no future, shamed when young and widowed when old. Your Maker is your husband, He tells her. Make your tent bigger. And at the end of all that comes this promise. Notice it does not say no weapon will be made. Weapons will be made. The promise is that they will not succeed. So the dream existing is not proof the verse has failed. And the last word matters: Heritage, not wages. It is spoken to a woman who was certain she had nothing to inherit and nothing to leave behind.

Isaiah does not ask the barren woman to pretend she is happy. He tells her to sing before anything has changed, and to make her tent bigger for children who have not arrived yet. That is a strange, brave kind of faith: preparing room for what God has promised while the room is still empty. Whatever feels empty in your life tonight, a hope that has not come, a fear that you will be left with nothing, hear this chapter as spoken to you. You are not forgotten. Your heritage is already set aside, and no weapon can take it.

15

1 John 4:4 ○

Little children, you are from God, and have conquered them; for the one who is in you is greater than the one who is in the world.

❀ ❀ ❀

Little children. John writes like a mother. Have conquered is a finished act with lasting results. He does not say you will win. He says you already have. Macrina was the older sister of Basil the Great and Gregory of Nyssa, and the head of a monastic community. She was dying, in pain, and Gregory sat beside her falling apart with grief. She spent her last strength arguing him out of his despair about death. He wrote the conversation down afterwards as On the Soul and the Resurrection and called her, all through it, simply the Teacher [A]. She was the weakest body in the room and the strongest mind in it. The comparison here is not you against the enemy. It is the One in you against the one in the world.

Gregory wrote his sister’s life so that the world would not forget her. He tells us that at the very end she made the sign of the cross over her eyes, her mouth and her heart, and that her last breath was a prayer [A]. She went into the deepest dark there is with her hand making the sign of the cross. You can do the same tonight. When fear comes, mark your forehead, your lips and your heart. It is not magic. It is a body remembering whose it is. The One who is in you is greater.

16

Romans 12:9 ○

Let love be genuine; hate what is evil, hold fast to what is good;

❀ ❀ ❀

It is strange to find this among the weapons, but it is one. The Greek word for hating evil means to recoil from it, the way you pull your hand back from something hot. And the two halves are one thing. A woman who cannot hate anything cannot really love anything either, because love that will never refuse is not love. It is just going along. Mary understood this. Her song in Luke 1, the Magnificat, is sung every day in the prayers of the Church, and it is tender and fierce in the same breath: He has scattered the proud, brought down the powerful, sent the rich away empty. She was probably in her teens. Keep your days clean and your yes and no clear, and the night has less to work with.

Mary’s song is part of the Church’s prayer every single day, and generations of women have sung it in convents and kitchens and hospital rooms. It is not a soft song. It is a song about God turning things right side up, sung by a young woman with no power at all. You do not have to be strong to hate evil and love good. You only have to be clear. Tonight, if confusion and fear blur everything together, ask for that clarity: let my yes be yes and my no be no, Lord, even in the dark.

17

Psalm 97:10 ○

The LORD loves those who hate evil; he guards the lives of his faithful; he rescues them from the hand of the wicked.

❀ ❀ ❀

Rescued from the hand of the wicked. Hand, not voice. You may still hear him. What he does not have is a grip. In 1 Kings 17 a widow in Sidon, a Gentile and unnamed, is down to a handful of flour and a little oil, gathering sticks to cook one last meal for herself and her son before they die. She feeds Elijah first, and the flour does not run out. Then her son dies anyway, and she is given him back a second time. Jesus names her in His first sermon. Death reached into that woman’s house twice, and twice its hand was opened. Isaac of Nineveh says God is nearest at the hour a person feels most abandoned [A]. The guarding is quiet. That does not make it partial.

The widow gave her last meal to a stranger before she knew it would come back to her. That is frightening generosity. And later, when her son died, she was angry and said so to the prophet’s face. Both of these are faith: giving when you have little, and complaining honestly when it hurts. God was not put off by either. If tonight you have little left, little sleep, little strength, little hope, give Him the little. The flour did not run out in her house. What you give Him tonight will not run out either.

Psalm 74:13

[LXX 73:13]

You divided the sea by

❀ ❀ ❀

Israel’s oldest fear was the sea monster. Chaos with a face. Something you cannot reason with. God does not negotiate with it. He breaks its heads at the founding of the world. And Scripture is not shy about who gets to finish such things. In Judges 4 the general Sisera, who had oppressed Israel for twenty years with nine hundred iron chariots, dies on the floor of a woman’s tent. Jael gives him milk, covers him, waits until he sleeps, and drives a tent peg through his head. Deborah, the judge who led Israel, had already said the honour would go to a woman. What visits you in sleep has a broken skull and is running on noise. Terror and power are not the same thing.

Deborah’s song after the battle calls Jael most blessed of women [A]. Centuries later Elizabeth would greet Mary with almost the same words. The Church has always noticed that echo: women blessed for their part in God’s victory over an enemy who seemed far too strong. That victory does not ask you to be violent. It asks you to trust that the thing frightening you is already beaten. The heads are already broken. When a dream shows you something terrible, remember that it is showing you a defeated thing, still making noise.

19

Psalm 68:2

[LXX 67:2]

As smoke is driven away, so drive them away; as wax melts before fire, let the wicked perish before God.

❀ ❀ ❀

Two pictures, and both weigh nothing. Smoke and wax. This is not a fight. It is a melting. There is nothing solid to push against. Amma Syncletica was a wealthy woman of Alexandria who gave everything away and taught a community of women in the desert. She used the same picture from the other side. People beginning the way to God, she said, are choked by smoke at first and it makes them weep, and they think the smoke is what is happening to them. In fact a fire is being lit [A]. Smoke is what fire makes before it gives heat. It stings, it blinds, it fills the room, and it is nothing at all. Fire does not fight wax. It arrives, and the shape is gone.

Syncletica knew suffering from the inside. In her old age she endured years of painful illness, including a disease of the mouth that took away her ability to speak, and the account of her life says she bore it without bitterness [A]. The woman who taught others about smoke and fire was tested by fire to the very end. Her suffering did not mean God had left her. It became part of her teaching. Your tears now are not proof that something has gone wrong. They may be the smoke of a fire being lit. Stay near it. The warmth comes.

20

Psalm 91:13

[LXX 90:13]

You will tread on the lion and the adder, the young lion and the serpent you will trample under foot.

❀ ❀ ❀

Four creatures. Lion and snake, young lion and adder. Open violence and hidden poison. The obvious and the sly. The best commentary ever written on this verse came from a young woman in a prison in Carthage around the year 203, writing about her own dream. Perpetua was twenty-two, a noblewoman, still nursing her baby, waiting to be killed for her faith, and part of the account that survives is her own prison diary, one of the earliest texts we have written by a Christian woman. She dreamt of a narrow bronze ladder to heaven hung with hooks and blades, and at the bottom an enormous dragon. She wrote: it will not harm me, in the name of Jesus Christ. Then she used its head as the first step. Under is the only place this verse puts them.

Perpetua wrote that when she woke from that dream she understood she would suffer, and she stopped putting her hope in this world [A]. She was not spared. But the dragon was under her feet the whole way. Her dream did not frighten her into giving up; it strengthened her for what came next. Not every dream is an enemy. Sometimes God gives a vision that shows the enemy where he belongs. Ask Him for one dream where the dragon is underfoot. Until then, say her words: it will not harm me, in the name of Jesus Christ.

21

John 16:33

I have said this to you, so that in me you may have peace. In the world you face persecution. But take courage; I have conquered the world!

❀ ❀ ❀

He said this hours before He was arrested. He speaks of a victory the world had not yet seen as something already finished. That is why your situation tonight is not a verdict on it. When Jesus was a baby, old Simeon told His mother in the temple that a sword would go through her own soul one day. Mary carried that sentence for thirty-three years. In John 19 she is standing at the Cross while nearly all the disciples have scattered. She did not have peace because things were peaceful. In me you may have peace. He gives you the place. Peace is not a mood you are failing to produce. It is somewhere you go, the way you walk into a church without first feeling worthy of the door.

Mary did not get an explanation at the Cross. She got three days of waiting. Peace for her was not understanding. It was staying near Him while everything was dark, and holding on to what He had said. The Church stands with her every Good Friday, and every year the third day comes. You do not have to understand your nights in order to have peace in them. Stay near. Hold on to what He has said: I have conquered the world. The third day came for her. It will come for you.

22

Exodus 14:14

The LORD will fight for you, and you have only to keep still.

❀ ❀ ❀

Israel is trapped. Water in front, chariots behind. And the command is to stop moving. Some frantic effort is itself the enemy’s work. The whole book of Exodus turns on a girl who understood this. In Exodus 2, before Moses has a name, his older sister stands at a distance by the river to see what will happen to him. She does not swim out. She does not shout. She stands still, watches, waits for the right second, and then speaks one sentence to Pharaoh’s daughter and saves him, and through him a nation. Being still is not doing nothing. It is trust that has stopped bargaining. On the nights you cannot pray well, this is the whole assignment.

Miriam was probably still a child when she stood watching that basket. She had no power at all. And her quick, brave words to a princess did more than save her brother. They brought him back into his own mother’s arms, because Pharaoh’s daughter paid Moses’ own mother to nurse him [A]. Watchful stillness can do what panic never could. You are watching over yourself tonight, and perhaps over others too. You do not have to do everything. Stand still. Watch. Wait. God will put the right word in your mouth when the moment comes.

23

Numbers 10:35

Whenever the ark set out, Moses would say, "Arise, O LORD, let your enemies be scattered, and your foes flee before you."

❀ ❀ ❀

Israel said this every single time the ark moved. A sentence worn smooth by repeating. That is exactly what you want in the dark: not beautiful words, but words your mouth already knows. Hannah understood how women keep faith over years. In 1 Samuel she gives her son Samuel to serve at the shrine at Shiloh, and then every year she makes him a little robe and carries it up to him, each one slightly bigger than the last. It is one of the plainest sentences in the Bible and one of the most moving. Faithfulness looks like that. Not a burst of feeling. A stitch repeated yearly. And notice the ark went ahead of them. It did not follow them in.

Every year that little robe was an act of love sent across a distance. Hannah could not keep Samuel with her. She could only keep showing up. Many women know that kind of love, the love that keeps sewing and sending when there is nothing else you can do. Your prayers at night are like that robe. They may feel small and repetitive, but they are carried up every time, and they fit. Make one short prayer your little robe tonight: Arise, O Lord, and go ahead of me.

24

Psalm 27:1

[LXX 26:1]

The LORD is my light and my salvation; whom

❀ ❀ ❀

The psalm answers fear with a question instead of an argument. Three names: light, salvation, stronghold. Not one of them is yours to supply. David does not pretend there is nothing to fear. He asks whom, and finds the list empty. Hagar did the same sum in worse conditions. Alone in a desert in Genesis 16, pregnant, with no home to go back to, she said: You are El-Roi, the God who sees me. Then she asked, half amazed, whether she had really just looked at the One who looks at her. No one in Scripture before her gives God a name. She had no stronghold. She had the fact of being seen, and it was enough to get her up and walking. Ask the question out loud tonight.

The well where Hagar met God was named after her words: Beer-lahai-roi, the well of the Living One who sees me. Years later Isaac, the son of the woman who had driven Hagar away, went to live beside that same well [A]. A slave girl’s meeting with God became a landmark for the whole family. The place where you are most afraid can become the place where you meet Him. One day you may look back on these nights and name them after what He did in them. For now, simply say it: You are the God who sees me.

Psalm 35:1

[LXX 34:1]

Contend, O LORD, with those who contend with me; fight against those who fight against me!

❀ ❀ ❀

David hands over the court case. The Hebrew word is legal language. Jesus told a story about exactly this in Luke 18 and made the hero a woman. She is a widow, which in that society meant no money, no legal standing and nobody to speak for her in court. She had one tactic: coming back. And she wore down a judge who feared neither God nor man. Luke says Jesus told it so that people would pray and not lose heart. You may be far too tired to argue your own defence at night. This verse says you were never the defence lawyer in the first place. Hand Him the case. Then sleep while the court is sitting, which is exactly what a client with a good lawyer is allowed to do.

Jesus told this story because He knew people would get tired of praying. He knew prayers would sometimes seem to go unanswered and that we would want to quit. The widow is His picture of someone who does not quit. And notice how He ends it: God will grant justice to His chosen ones who cry to Him day and night, and will do it quickly [A]. Day and night. Your night prayers are inside that promise. Keep coming back. You are not bothering Him. You are doing exactly what He asked.

Psalm 3:7

[LXX 3:8]

Rise up, O LORD! Deliver me, O my God! For you strike all my enemies on the cheek; you break the teeth of the wicked.

❀ ❀ ❀

A struck cheek and broken teeth. The mouth disarmed. David wrote it while running from his own son, and the same psalm says he lay down and slept. There is a scene where this happens to a woman. In John 8 they drag her into the temple court and stand her in the middle as bait for a trap set for Jesus; the man involved is never mentioned. Every mouth in that circle was already loaded. Jesus wrote in the dust and said one sentence, and the whole ring of accusers walked away without speaking, starting with the oldest. Not one of them ever said what they had come to say. Whatever speaks to you in dreams is described here as something that can no longer bite and can no longer form words.

After the accusers had gone, Jesus did something the crowd never saw. He looked at her and asked: where are they, has no one condemned you? She said: no one, Lord. And He said: neither do I condemn you; go [A]. The last voice she heard that day was His, and it was not accusing. Many women carry voices of accusation for years, things said to them and things said about them, and some of those voices come back in dreams. Let His be the last voice tonight. No one is condemning you. Go, and sleep.

Psalm 44:5

[LXX 43:6]

Through you we push down our foes; through your name we tread down our assailants.

❀ ❀ ❀

Through you, and through your name. Twice the psalm refuses to put the strength in the people doing the treading. And yet they do tread. It is not done for them while they watch. The woman in Mark 5 shows how this works. She had bled for twelve years, which under the law made her permanently unclean, so nobody was allowed to touch her or anything she touched. She had spent everything on doctors. She reached through a crowd and took hold of the edge of His clothes. Power went out of Him. But it was her hand. And He stopped the whole procession to call her Daughter in public. This is what the Fathers call working together with God. All of it His power. Really your hand.

Think how much courage it took for her to reach out. For twelve years she had been told that her body made her unclean and that touching anyone was forbidden. She expected to be scolded. Instead she was called Daughter, in front of everybody, and told to go in peace [A]. Many women carry shame about their bodies that was put on them by others. Jesus does not share it. When you reach for Him tonight, do not wait until you feel clean enough or calm enough. Reach as you are. He calls you Daughter.

Psalm 6:8

[LXX 6:9]

Depart from me, all you workers of evil, for the LORD has heard the sound of my weeping.

❀ ❀ ❀

This is the verse where the psalm turns. For seven verses he weeps. Then, with nothing changed around him, he gives an order. The only thing that happened is that he realised he had been heard. Hannah is the model. In 1 Samuel 1 she prays in the shrine with her lips moving and no sound coming out, which was unusual enough that the priest Eli assumed she was drunk and said so to her face. She explained herself, and he gave her one sentence of blessing. Scripture says she went away and ate, and her face was no longer sad. Nothing had changed yet. No child, no promise, no sign. She simply knew she had been heard. That is where weeping stops, and it stops first.

Eli thought Hannah was drunk because her prayer was silent and deep. Sometimes the people who should understand you best get it wrong. Hannah did not argue at length. She said: I have been pouring out my soul before the LORD [A]. Then she let it go. You may have people in your life who do not understand these nights, or who misread your tiredness or your tears. You do not owe them a long explanation. Your prayer has been heard by the One who matters. Like Hannah, you can eat, rest, and let your face be lighter.

Psalm 18:2

[LXX 17]

The LORD is my rock, my fortress, and my deliverer, my God, my rock in whom I take refuge, my shield, and the horn of my salvation, my stronghold.

❀ ❀ ❀

Seven names piled one on another, because one was not enough for the night he was having. Some prayer works by piling up rather than by arguing. This is that kind, and it is how the women of your own tradition have always prayed. The Syriac hymns to the Mother of God, Ephrem’s and the ones in the West Syriac office, do nothing else: ark, ladder, gate, fleece, burning bush, harbour, tower, lamp, title after title, because one would not hold her either. You were taught to pray in lists before you were taught to pray in sentences. Say all seven slowly tonight. By the last one your breathing will have changed. That is not a trick. It is part of how the body prays.

Try this tonight. Instead of thinking about the fear, start naming God the way this psalm does, slowly, one name at a time. My rock. My fortress. My deliverer. My refuge. My shield. My salvation. My stronghold. If you lose your place, begin again. If you remember a title of the Mother of God from the hymns of your childhood, add it. A frightened mind cannot hold a long argument, but it can hold a name. And every name is a door you can walk through.

Psalm 23:4

[LXX 22]

Even though I walk through the darkest valley, I fear no evil; for you are with me; your rod and your staff, they comfort me.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Hebrew is death-shadow. A shadow only exists if there is light behind it. And watch the change. For three verses the psalm talks about the shepherd. Here it turns and talks to him. You are with me. Fear did that. The Shunammite woman in 2 Kings 4 did the same. She was a wealthy woman, unnamed, who built a room on her roof for Elisha. Her son, born after years of childlessness, died suddenly in her lap at noon. She laid him on the prophet’s bed, shut the door, saddled the donkey, and answered everyone who asked her on the road with two words: it is well. She was not pretending. She refused to discuss it with anyone but the one person who could act.

The prophet did not come at first. He sent his servant with his staff, and nothing happened. The Shunammite refused to go home without Elisha himself. She said: as the LORD lives, I will not leave you [A]. And he got up and went with her. Sometimes the first help does not work, and you are tempted to give up. Do not settle for anything less than God Himself. Hold on to Him the way she held on to the prophet. Her son was given back, alive. You are not walking this valley alone.

Psalm 70:1

[LXX 69]

Be pleased, O God, to deliver me. O LORD, make haste to help me!

❀ ❀ ❀

Cassian says the desert fathers made this single verse their constant prayer, saying it over every trouble at every hour [A]. It is short enough to say while frightened, and that is the point. No theology. No build-up. Just hurry. Heaven has never found that rude. In the Syriac Church this was women’s work as much as anyone’s. The bnāt qyāmā, the Daughters of the Covenant, were women who vowed themselves to God but lived in their own towns rather than in monasteries. They stood in choir in Edessa and Nisibis and sang the daily office through the night for centuries [A]. Short verses, repeated endlessly, by women who were tired. You are not inventing a method. You are joining one.

These women were not famous. We know almost none of their names. But Ephrem trusted them to sing the faith of the Church, and they did it night after night for generations [A]. And they stood together. They did not pray alone. If you have a mother, a sister or a friend who prays, ask her to say this short verse with you, or for you, at the same hour. O God, make haste to help me. Two women saying the same words in two different houses is a choir.

Psalm 119:11

[LXX 118]

I treasure your word in my heart, so that I may not sin against you.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Hebrew word for treasure means to hide, to store away. What you have memorised is out of the dark’s reach, because you cannot lose it by being unable to find the book at three in the morning. This is why Ephrem wrote the way he did. In fourth-century Nisibis and Edessa he put doctrine into hymns called madrāshē and trained choirs of women to sing them in public, so that the faith lived in women’s memories and women’s voices. Jacob of Serugh praises him for this specifically in his homily on Ephrem [A]. He trusted women to carry the theology of the Church in their heads. Learn five of these. Five is enough. They will be in you on the nights when you are not in yourself.

Jacob of Serugh wrote that Ephrem saw the women of the Church kept silent, and decided it was right that they should lift their voices in praise [A]. Think of that. In the fourth century, in your own tradition, women were trusted to hold doctrine in their memory and sing it aloud. You stand in that line. The verses you learn now may one day be the ones you sing over someone else: a child, a friend, a sick parent. What you store up in the dark becomes what you give away in the light.

Genesis 3:15

I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; he will strike your head, and you will strike his heel.

❀ ❀ ❀

The first promise in the Bible is spoken in the enemy’s hearing, before Adam has even been told the consequences. And it is a promise about a woman and her child. Her offspring. From the second century the Church read Eve and Mary together: Irenaeus, in Against Heresies, says the knot Eve tied by disobedience Mary untied by obedience, so that what a woman bound a woman loosed [A]. The serpent went to a woman first because he thought she was the weak point. He was answered through a woman. He gets a heel. He loses his head. It was never a fair trade, and he has known it since the day it was said over him while he lay in the dust in front of her.

Eve’s story is not the end of women’s story. It is the beginning of a promise that runs through Sarah, Hannah and Elizabeth, all the way to Mary. Ephrem sings that Eve and Mary are like the world’s two eyes: one was darkened and blind, and the other is clear and full of light [A]. You stand on the side of the bright eye. Whatever the serpent whispers to you at night, remember that his head has been under a woman’s heel since the third chapter of the Bible.

Exodus 15:3

The LORD is a warrior; the LORD is his name.

❀ ❀ ❀

The first victory song in the Bible belongs to a woman. In Exodus 15 Moses sings, and then Miriam picks up a tambourine and all the women go out after her with tambourines and dancing, and she sings it back to them on the far shore. The text calls her a prophet, the first woman given that title in Scripture, and her song is the oldest poetry in the Bible. This is a song for people who have already crossed. The whole skill is singing it in the present tense while you are still in the water. And notice what she needed. Not strength. An instrument in her hand and other women around her. If there is a woman who prays with you, tonight is the night to tell her so.

Miriam had waited a long time for that song. If she was the girl by the river, then her whole life ran between two waters: the Nile, where she watched her baby brother, and the sea, where she danced on the far shore as an old woman. God remembered both. Your story is not finished either. The fear you are standing in tonight is not the last water. Pick up your tambourine anyway, even if it is only one line hummed under your breath. The LORD is a warrior. He fights for women who sing before they are safe.

Deuteronomy 31:6

Be strong and bold; have no fear or dread of them, because it is the LORD your God who goes with you; he will not fail you or forsake you.

❀ ❀ ❀

Moses says this at the end of his life to people going into a land he will never see. The Hebrew behind forsake means to loosen your grip. He will not let go. The promise is about His hand, not your grip, which is a mercy, because yours is the one that gets tired. Naomi thought she had been let go. In Ruth 1 she comes home to Bethlehem having buried her husband and both sons, and tells the whole town not to call her Naomi, which means pleasant, but Mara, which means bitter, because God had dealt bitterly with her. She was allowed to say it. Nobody corrected her. Four chapters later she is holding a grandson.

Notice who brings Naomi back to life. First Ruth, a young widow who refused to leave her. Then the women of Bethlehem, who say to her: blessed be the LORD, who has not left you without family [A]. Naomi felt forsaken, and God answered her through women who stayed. If you feel bitter tonight, you are allowed to say so, as she did. But watch for the people He sends to stay with you. They are part of His not letting go. You were never meant to hold on alone.

Joshua 1:9

I hereby command you: Be strong and courageous; do not be frightened or dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go.

❀ ❀ ❀

Courage arrives here as a command. That means it is not a feeling you have to find inside yourself first. You can obey an order you do not feel. Esther proves it. She was a Jewish orphan raised by her cousin Mordecai, who became queen of Persia, and going to the king without being summoned carried the death penalty. She did not feel brave. She fasted three days and three nights first, and the sentence she finally produced was not a confident one: I will go to the king, and if I die, I die. That is courage as a decision made before the feeling arrives. It is the only kind that has ever existed. Sleep is a place you go.

Esther did not fast alone. She asked her cousin and all the Jews of the city to fast with her, and her own maids fasted alongside her [A]. Courage grew in a circle of people praying together before it showed itself in one woman walking into a throne room. When you face something frightening, you do not have to find your courage alone. Ask for prayer. Tell someone what you are afraid of. And hold on to the words her cousin gave her: who knows whether you have come here for such a time as this.

2 Kings 6:16

He replied, “Do not be afraid, for there are more with us than there are with them.”

❀ ❀ ❀

Elisha’s servant saw the enemy army and panicked. The horses of fire had been there the whole time, unseen. Nothing in that valley changed when his eyes were opened. Only his counting. The women walking to the tomb before dawn had the same experience. Mark records the question they asked each other on the road: who will roll the stone away for us, because it was very large and they were a few women with spices and no strength for it. They arrived, and it was done. The thing they had dreaded the whole way had been settled before they left home. Pray Elisha’s prayer tonight: Lord, open her eyes. The help is not on its way. It is already here.

The women did not know the stone was gone, and they went anyway. That is the whole lesson. If they had waited until they had solved the problem, they would have stayed at home and missed the Resurrection. Some of your worries tonight are like that stone: large, heavy, and already moved. You cannot see that yet because you are still on the road. Keep walking. Keep praying. When morning comes, you may find that what you feared most was dealt with while you were still worrying about it.

2 Chronicles 20:15

Do not fear or be dismayed at this great multitude; for the battle is not yours but God’s.

❀ ❀ ❀

King Jehoshaphat answers an invasion by appointing singers and sending them out in front of the army. That is not a figure of speech. It is what the chapter says. In the Bible, praise often comes first and the outcome second. The clearest case is a teenage girl in a village, pregnant, unmarried, with every social disaster still ahead of her. Her song in Luke 1 is in the past tense throughout, as though everything in it had already happened: He has scattered the proud, He has brought down the mighty. Nothing had been brought down. She sang it anyway, and she was right. If you can sing one line before sleep tonight, the front rank is in place.

Mary sang in the past tense because she trusted God’s promise more than what she could see. That is not pretending. It is faith speaking ahead of the evidence. You can do this too. Before you sleep tonight, say one sentence about your fear as if God has already answered it: He has kept me through the night; He has driven the fear away. You are not lying. You are agreeing in advance with what He has promised. Jehoshaphat’s singers went out first, and the battle was won before a sword was raised [A].

Proverbs 18:10

The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous run into it and are safe.

❀ ❀ ❀

The name. Not an argument about God. Not a feeling about God. The Name. And the righteous run into it, because a tower only helps if you go in, and going in takes seconds. This is why the Jesus Prayer is short. There is no time to compose a speech while running. The Church remembers Barbara, a girl of the third or fourth century shut in a tower by her father to keep her away from the world, and killed by him when he found she had become a Christian. While he was away she had a third window cut into the wall so the light came in three times, for the Trinity, and told him why to his face. A tower can be a cell or a refuge. It depends whose name is on it.

Barbara’s father shut her away because he wanted to control her life and keep her for himself. Many women know some version of that pressure: someone else’s idea of who they should be, whom they should belong to, what they should believe. Barbara made the tower a place of prayer instead of a prison. You cannot always choose your walls. You can choose whose name is written on them. Write His name tonight on the walls of your room, if only in your heart. The name of the LORD is a strong tower. Run in.

Isaiah 27:1

On that day the LORD with his cruel and great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent, Leviathan the twisting serpent, and he will kill the dragon that is in the sea.

❀ ❀ ❀

Isaiah names the dragon twice, fleeing and twisting, as if to use up all its escapes. And the sword in this verse belongs to the Lord alone. No human hand appears anywhere in it. There are things it is not your job to kill. The Syriac poets understood this. In the soghyāthā, the dialogue poems still sung in the West Syriac office, Mary stands at the Cross and speaks and weeps and asks questions, and she does not fight [A]. She stayed. That was harder. The disciples ran. She stood. Standing is not the same as doing nothing, and it is not giving up. It is refusing to leave a place you were given. Tonight your job is not to kill anything.

Mothers know what it costs to watch someone they love suffer when they cannot stop it. It is one of the hardest things a person can do, and Mary did it in public, at an execution, while soldiers gambled for His clothes. She could not hold the sword. She could stay. Standing near is a form of love and a form of faith. You may not be able to defeat the thing that frightens you. That is His work, and His sword is already drawn. Your part is simpler: stand near Him, and do not run.

Isaiah 41:10

Do not fear, for I am with you, do not be afraid, for I am your God; I will strengthen you, I will help you, I will uphold you with my victorious right hand.

❀ ❀ ❀

Two commands and then four promises, and every promise begins with I. I am, I will strengthen, I will help, I will uphold. You are the one being acted on the whole way through. On nights when you can do nothing at all, this verse asks nothing of you except to be held. And eight chapters later, in Isaiah 49, the same prophet reaches for the strongest comparison he can find: can a woman forget the baby at her breast, or feel nothing for the child of her own body? Even she might forget. I will not forget you. God chose a mother’s body as the nearest picture of His own faithfulness, and then said even that is too weak.

In that same chapter God also says: I have written you on the palms of my hands [A]. A name written on a hand cannot be forgotten, because you see it every time you reach for anything. Many women have written a name or a number on a palm so as not to lose it. God uses that homely picture of Himself. Your name is on His hands. When you cannot hold on tonight, remember that holding is His part. You are carried by the One who cannot forget you, not even for a moment.

Jeremiah 20:11

But the LORD is with me like a dread warrior; therefore my persecutors will stumble, and they will not prevail.

❀ ❀ ❀

Jeremiah says this in the same chapter where he curses the day he was born. Faith and despair sat in one man on one day, and the book keeps both. The same prophet records the most unconsoled line in Scripture. In Jeremiah 31 Rachel, Jacob’s wife, who died in childbirth, is pictured weeping from her grave for the exiles passing by, and refusing to be comforted, because her children are gone. God does not scold her for refusing. He answers her: keep your voice from weeping, there is a reward, there is hope for your future. If confidence and terror are both inside you tonight, you are in the company of a prophet, and of a mother God answered without ever telling her to stop crying.

Matthew remembers Rachel again when Herod kills the little boys of Bethlehem [A]. Her weeping echoes through the whole Bible, and God never tells her that her grief is wrong. If you are crying at night, you are not weak and you are not failing at faith. Rachel’s tears were heard. The promise to her was: there is hope for your future. Your tears are not wasted either. Let them come if they come. Then let Jeremiah’s other words come too: the LORD is with me like a dread warrior.

Micah 7:8

Do not rejoice over me, O my enemy; when I fall, I shall rise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me.

❀ ❀ ❀

When I fall. Not if. The verse allows for the fall and promises the rising in the same breath. And look where the light is. Not instead of the darkness. Inside it. The Lord becomes light to someone who is still sitting in the dark. Gregory of Nyssa called this the luminous darkness, and he learned to say it at his sister’s bedside. The whole of his book On the Soul and the Resurrection is a conversation at Macrina’s deathbed, and he is the one who needs consoling throughout it. She took his grief apart piece by piece and lifted the subject off her own body onto the resurrection, until he stopped crying. She did not get up again. She rose anyway.

Gregory later wrote about Moses meeting God in thick darkness on the mountain, and said that the darkness was where God was most truly found [A]. He had first learned this beside his sister. Darkness is not always the absence of God. Sometimes it is the place where He waits. When you wake in the dark tonight, do not assume you are alone in it. Say Micah’s words: when I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me. You may not see the light with your eyes. You can still be sitting in it.

Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, says the LORD of hosts.

❀ ❀ ❀

Spoken to a man rebuilding a ruin without enough resources. The verse does not promise him strength. It moves the source. Whatever you cannot manage tonight by effort was never going to be managed by effort. And by my spirit sounds different in your own tradition than in Greek or Latin. In early Syriac the word for Spirit, Rūḥā, is feminine, and Aphrahat and Ephrem both speak of the Spirit in motherly terms [A]. The verb in Genesis 1 for the Spirit over the waters is the verb for a bird sitting over a nest. The power that will do what you cannot is described, in the language your Church prays in, as something that hovers and warms. Not a force. A covering.

Early Syriac writers compared the Spirit’s care to a mother who warms and feeds her children [A]. You do not have to feel strong for the Spirit to be at work in you. Psalm 16:8

[LXX 15]

I keep the LORD always before me; because he is at my right hand, I shall not be moved.

❀ ❀ ❀

I keep. The one active verb here is yours, and it is small: keep Him in view. Everything else follows from that. Mary of Bethany is the whole commentary. In Luke 10 she sits down at His feet and listens while her sister works. Sitting at a teacher’s feet was the posture of a disciple, and it was not the expected place for a woman. When she was criticised for it, with a fair complaint, Jesus said she had chosen the one needed thing and it would not be taken from her. The needed thing was not a task. It was where she was looking. On the day after a bad night, when you are too tired to achieve anything, this verse is still completely within reach.

Later, when her brother died, Mary of Bethany went out and fell at Jesus’ feet again, this time weeping [A]. Her place was always at His feet: listening there, grieving there, and at last pouring perfume there. She found one place and kept returning to it. Find your one place tonight. Keeping Him in view is enough.

Psalm 17:8

[LXX 16]

Guard me as the apple of the eye; hide me in the shadow of your wings.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Hebrew behind apple of the eye is ishon bat ʿayin. It is the ordinary Hebrew name for the pupil, and it means the little figure, or the daughter of the eye. It is the tiny reflection of yourself you can see in someone’s pupil when you are standing close enough. To be kept as the apple of His eye is to be held that near, and that visibly. Every woman knows the difference between being looked at and being seen. You learn it early and you never lose the ability to tell which is happening. This verse is about the second. You are not being watched. You are being held in a gaze close enough that your face is in it. And then the verse adds wings.

The same picture appears in Deuteronomy. God finds His people in a howling wilderness and keeps them as the apple of His eye, like an eagle that stirs up its nest and spreads its wings over its young [A]. Wilderness, eye, wings: all in one breath. God’s watching is not cold. It is the close, tender watching of a parent over a child in a dangerous place. Tonight you may feel watched by something that frightens you. Turn to the other gaze, the One that keeps you, close enough that your face is in it.

Psalm 18:29

[LXX 17]

By you I can crush a troop, and by my God I can leap over a wall.

❀ ❀ ❀

Two pictures, one violent and one almost playful. A wall is what shuts you in, and the psalm does not say the wall comes down. It says you get over it. Rahab lived in one. She was a prostitute in Jericho who hid Israel’s spies, and Joshua 2 tells us her house was built into the city wall itself. That is how she could let them down through her window on a rope, and why the single scarlet cord hanging from it could be seen from outside. She and her household were the only ones spared, and Matthew names her in the family tree of Christ. The wall that shut her in became her way out, and then her signal.

Rahab had a past people talked about, and Scripture keeps the label. But the New Testament names her twice more: in Hebrews as a woman of faith, and in James as someone whose actions proved her trust [A]. The label was not the last word. Whatever people have said about you, and whatever the dark says about you at night, God has the final word on your name. The scarlet cord in her window was a sign of rescue. Hang your own sign out tonight: a short prayer, a verse, the sign of the cross. Then rest.

Psalm 20:7

[LXX 19]

Some take pride in chariots, and some in horses, but our pride is in the name of the LORD our God.

❀ ❀ ❀

The contrast is not weak against strong. It is countable against named. You can count chariots. You cannot count the Name. Deborah faced this exact sum. She was a prophet and a judge, the only woman to lead Israel, and people came to her for judgement under a palm tree between Ramah and Bethel. Sisera had nine hundred iron chariots and twenty years of oppression behind him, and Barak would not go unless she came too. She went. Her song afterwards does not boast about tactics. It says the stars fought from their courses and a river swept them away. Our own age trusts its own chariots: the plan, the explanation, the diagnosis. Keep them. Just do not make them your pride.

Deborah said to Barak: has not the LORD gone out before you [A]? She did not tell him he was strong enough. She told him who was going ahead. That is the kind of encouragement worth giving and worth receiving. Many women are the Deborahs of their families, the ones who steady everyone else. Tonight it is your turn to hear her words. You do not have to face what frightens you with your own strength. The LORD has gone out before you. The chariots were real, and they were swept away.

Psalm 25:15

[LXX 24]

My eyes are ever toward the LORD, for he will pluck my feet out of the net.

❀ ❀ ❀

The eyes look up. The feet are caught. Both are true at once, and the psalm sees no problem in that. You can be genuinely trapped and genuinely looking at God in the same hour. The woman at the well in John 4 proves it. She came at noon, the hottest hour, which means she was avoiding the other women of the town, and her history was a net she could not get out of. She is the first person in John’s Gospel to whom Jesus says plainly who He is, and her whole town believed because of what she told them. The Orthodox Church calls her Photini. He did not wait for her feet to be free before He spoke.

After she met Jesus, the Samaritan woman left her water jar at the well and ran back into town [A]. She forgot the very thing she had come for, because meeting Him had changed what mattered. The Church remembers her as Photini, the enlightened one, and tradition says she went on to preach and to die as a martyr. The woman who came at noon to avoid people became someone who ran towards them. Fear may be making you hide right now. That will not be the end of your story. Keep your eyes on Him.

Psalm 27:3

[LXX 26]

Though an army encamp against me, my heart shall not fear; though war rise up against me, yet I will be confident.

❀ ❀ ❀

An army encamped. Settled in, not passing through. This is the prayer for a fear that has moved in and stayed. And the woman who held a position longest in the Bible is Rizpah. She was a concubine of King Saul, and after his death her two sons were executed and left unburied. In 2 Samuel 21 she takes sackcloth, spreads it on a rock, and stays there from the start of harvest until the rains come, driving birds off their bodies by day and wild animals off by night. A whole dry season. Alone. In the open. And the king heard what she was doing and buried them properly. David does not say the siege has lifted. He says his heart will not follow it.

Rizpah never says a word in the whole story. She does not argue with the king or make speeches. She simply stays, night after night, and her faithful watching moves the king to do what is right [A]. Quiet endurance has power. Many women keep watch in ways nobody notices: beside sick beds, over children, over ageing parents, over their own hearts. If you are keeping a long watch right now, God sees it. Your heart does not have to follow the siege. Stay on your rock. The rain will come.

Psalm 31:4

[LXX 30]

Take me out of the net that is hidden for me, for you are my refuge.

❀ ❀ ❀

Hidden is the word that matters. Most of what frightens you at night is what you cannot see the shape of. There is a night scene in Song of Songs 5 where the bride goes out into the city looking for the man she loves. The watchmen, the very men posted to protect her, find her, beat her, and take away her shawl. Scripture does not soften it and does not explain it. It simply records that a woman went out at night to look for love and was hurt by the guards. The psalm does not ask for the trap to be explained. Only for the feet to be lifted out. You can be rescued from something nobody ever explains to you.

The Song does not leave the bride beaten and alone. In the very next chapter she still says: I am my beloved’s and my beloved is mine [A]. Her love survived the harm. Some women have been hurt by people who should have protected them, and those memories can return at night as frightening dreams. If that is part of your story, it is real, and God does not ask you to pretend otherwise; it is also worth telling someone safe. But it is not the whole story. You belong to Someone who does not hurt you. Hold that sentence tonight.

Psalm 34:7

[LXX 33]

The angel of the LORD encamps around those who fear him, and delivers them.

❀ ❀ ❀

The verb means to pitch camp. To stay, not to visit. And around, not beside. A circle, with no open side. The ʿirē, the Wakeful Ones, keep the hours you cannot keep [A]. The Church has an old instinct about this, and it is a motherly one. From Constantinople to Kerala the picture that stayed is the one from the vision of the Protection: the Mother of God spreading her veil in prayer over a frightened congregation at night during a siege. A veil is not armour. It is what a woman lays over a sleeping child. It works by being there, not by force. The night office is divided into watches for the same reason, so no hour of the dark is left without someone awake in it.

The feast of the Protection remembers frightened people praying through the night in a church, with an enemy outside the city walls. That is very close to where you are. The veil did not remove the danger in that moment. It covered the people while the danger passed. Tonight, picture that veil over your bed, over your room, over your thoughts. Mothers cover their children when they sleep, not because a blanket can fight danger, but because love wants to be close. The angel of the LORD encamps around you. You are covered.

Psalm 46:1

[LXX 45]

God is our refuge and strength, a very present help in trouble.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Hebrew behind very present means found, available, right there. And the next verses describe mountains falling into the sea, so this is not a calm scene. Help that is found in trouble is better than help that prevents it, because it meets you where you actually are. When Mary was given the most frightening news any young woman has ever received, the angel also told her about Elizabeth, her older relative, pregnant with John the Baptist after years of childlessness. Mary got up and went quickly into the hill country and stayed three months. Two pregnant women in one house, one very young and one very old, both carrying something they could not explain. That is what present help looked like. It had a face, and a spare room.

When Mary arrived, Elizabeth’s child leapt in her womb and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit [A]. Help came to Mary through another woman’s joy. Neither of them was in an easy situation. One was too young, one was too old, and both were carrying something that would change everything. Yet together they sang. You may need an Elizabeth right now, someone who will welcome you without questions and bless what God is doing in you. Ask God to show you who she is. And be an Elizabeth to someone else when you can.

Psalm 51:10

[LXX 50]

Create in me a clean heart, O God, and put a new and right spirit within me.

❀ ❀ ❀

Create is a special Hebrew verb. It is only ever used of God, and only for making something out of nothing. David is not asking for a repair. And the Bible is not shy about where God prefers to work. Sarah past ninety. Rebekah. Rachel. Hannah. Samson’s mother. Elizabeth in old age. Five and more women told they could not have children, and the line of promise runs straight through them. Every one of those stories is the same verb: something out of nothing, again and again, on purpose. If what is inside you tonight feels like nothing to work with, you have reached for exactly the right word. Nothing is not a problem for this verb. Nothing is what it prefers.

Each of those women prayed in a different way. Sarah laughed. Rachel demanded. Hannah wept. Elizabeth waited quietly for decades. God answered every one of them. There is no single correct way to bring your emptiness to Him. Some nights you may almost laugh at your own fear, some nights you may cry, some nights you may only wait. All of these are prayers He hears. Ask Him tonight to do what only He can do: to create, out of nothing, a clean heart and a new and steady spirit within you.

Psalm 54:4

[LXX 53]

But surely, God is my helper; the Lord is the upholder of my life.

❀ ❀ ❀

But surely. The psalm turns on one small word. Everything before it was true and distressing, and none of it is taken back. Faith here is not denying the first half of the sentence. It is adding the second. Martha has the best example. In John 11 her brother has been dead four days, and she goes out to meet Jesus on the road before He reaches the village while her sister stays in the house. She is past being polite. The first thing she says is a complaint: if you had been here, he would not have died. Then, without pausing: but even now I know God will give you whatever you ask. Both halves in one breath, neither cancelling the other.

After Martha’s but even now, Jesus said to her: I am the resurrection and the life; do you believe this? And she answered with one of the greatest confessions in the Gospels: yes, Lord, I believe that you are the Messiah, the Son of God [A]. That confession came from a grieving, frustrated woman who had just complained to Him. Your complaints do not disqualify your faith. Say both halves tonight. Lord, this is hard and I do not understand it. But even now, I trust you. Surely God is my helper.

Psalm 56:3–4

[LXX 55]

When I am afraid, I put my trust in you. In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust; I am not afraid; what can flesh do to me?

❀ ❀ ❀

When I am afraid. Not if. David plans his trust for the moment of fear instead of waiting for the fear to pass. And the oldest manuscripts of Mark end the Gospel on exactly this note, at Mark 16:8. The women ran from the tomb shaking and said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid. That is the last line of the book. The first witnesses of the Resurrection were terrified women who ran away, and everything we know came from them later, when they did tell it. Being afraid did not disqualify them and did not undo what they had seen. What can flesh do to me is asked by a man in real danger of being killed. He is weighing the body against what cannot be touched.

The other Gospels tell us what happened next. Their fear turned to great joy, and Jesus Himself met them on the road and said: do not be afraid [A]. He did not scold them for being frightened. He met them inside it. Fear is not the opposite of faith; turning away from Him is. You can be afraid and still be faithful. Tonight, when the fear rises, say David’s words out loud: when I am afraid, I put my trust in you. Being afraid and trusting can happen in the same breath.

Psalm 57:1

[LXX 56]

Be merciful to me, O God, be merciful to me, for in you my soul takes refuge; in the shadow of your wings I will take refuge, until the destroying storms pass by.

❀ ❀ ❀

The shadow of wings is a bird’s posture over her chicks. Shelter and warmth and blindness all at once, because underneath them you cannot see the storm either. That is part of the mercy. The Hebrew word kānāf means both a wing and the corner of a coat, and Ruth used that on purpose. She was a Moabite widow who had left her own people to stay with her mother-in-law, and she had no protector. Lying at Boaz’s feet at midnight on a threshing floor she said: spread your kānāf over your servant. He had blessed her weeks before for coming under the wings of the God of Israel, and she handed his own words back as a request. Jesus later used the same picture of Himself, and chose a hen gathering her chicks.

Ruth’s request at midnight was a risk. She was a foreign widow, alone with a man at night, putting her whole future into his hands. Boaz answered kindly and protected her good name [A]. God’s answer to your vulnerability is kinder still. When you feel exposed and unprotected at night, hear what Christ said over Jerusalem: how often have I desired to gather your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings [A]. A hen does not fight the storm. She covers. Get under the wings tonight, and stay there until the storm passes.

Psalm 61:2–3

[LXX 60]

From the end of the earth I call to you, when my heart is faint. Lead me to the rock that is higher than I; for you are my refuge, a strong tower against the enemy.

❀ ❀ ❀

When my heart is faint. The prayer is written for the times you cannot climb. And it says lead me, not show me. She asks to be taken, not instructed. The turning point of Mary of Egypt’s life, as Sophronius of Jerusalem tells it, happened at a church door in Jerusalem. Three times the crowd carried her forward and three times something stopped her, and she stood in the courtyard and understood why. She looked up at an icon of the Mother of God and asked her, one woman to another, to stand surety for her. Then she went in, and afterwards walked out into the desert for the rest of her life. She did not climb. She asked to be led by someone who had been that way.

After she came out of that church, Mary of Egypt heard a voice telling her that if she crossed the Jordan she would find rest [A]. She asked for direction and she received it. That is what lead me means. You do not need to see the whole road tonight. You need one step. Ask the Mother of God, as Mary did, to stand beside you and take you to her Son. Then ask Him for the next step only. Lead me to the rock that is higher than I. The rock was always there. She simply needed someone to show her the way to it.

Psalm 62:1–2

[LXX 61]

For God alone my soul waits in silence; from him comes my salvation. He alone is my rock and my salvation, my fortress; I shall never be shaken.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Hebrew word is a stillness that is itself the waiting, not a pause before it. Our instinct in fear is noise: explanation, reassurance, a lit screen, anyone’s voice. This psalm makes silence the active form of hope. Luke says it twice about Mary, once after the shepherds and once after finding Jesus in the temple, as if once were not enough: she treasured all these things and turned them over in her heart. Not understanding them. Not resolving them. Holding them unprocessed for years, including the parts that frightened her. Women are very often expected to explain themselves on demand. You are not required to produce an explanation tonight, to anyone, including yourself.

Mary’s silence was not empty. It was full of things she was holding until they made sense, and some of them she held for thirty years. You do not need to make sense of these nights tonight. You can hold them in your heart, unexplained, and trust that God will one day show you what they meant, or heal them without explaining. For God alone my soul waits in silence. Try that now: lie still, breathe slowly, say His name once, and wait, without needing to fix anything at all.

Psalm 91:1–2

[LXX 90]

You who live in the shelter of the Most High, who abide in the shadow of the Almighty, will say to the LORD, “My refuge and my fortress; my God, in whom I trust.”

❀ ❀ ❀

Four names for God in two verses: Most High, Almighty, LORD, my God. The psalm moves on purpose from the most distant title to the most personal one. So did He. In the Hymns on the Nativity Ephrem returns again and again to this: the One the heavens could not contain made Himself small enough to be carried by a girl, and was fed, and kept warm [A]. The One you are asking to shelter you tonight has been sheltered by a woman and knows that arrangement from both sides. And will say is future tense. You may not be able to say it yet tonight. The psalm expects that and waits for you.

Ephrem imagines Mary marvelling that she carries the One who carries everything, and nurses the One who feeds the world [A]. She must have asked the question you may ask tonight: how can someone so small be held by Someone so great? And yet it is true. The Most High allowed Himself to be held. He knows what it is to be small, carried and fed. Now He shelters you. When you cannot say the whole psalm, say only its last two words. My God. That is the most personal name of all.

Psalm 91:5–6

[LXX 90]

You will not fear the terror of the night, or the arrow that flies by day, or the pestilence that stalks in darkness, or the destruction that wastes at noonday.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Greek version calls the noonday destruction the noonday demon, and that is where the Fathers got their name for the heaviness that attacks when nothing at all is happening [A]. But the clause that belongs to you is the first one: The terror of the night. Scripture has a category for what is happening to you. You are not making it up and you are not the first. Amma Theodora was a desert mother in Egypt whom bishops consulted, and several of her ten surviving sayings are about discouragement. She taught that the body’s weakness and the enemy’s pressure often arrive together and should not be anxiously sorted out. Endure, she said, and call it what it is. Some nights are simply night, and this psalm counts those too.

Amma Theodora also said that trees which have not stood through the storms of winter cannot bear fruit, and that this life is like such a storm [A]. Your nights may be a kind of winter: hard, cold and long, with nothing seeming to grow. But roots grow in winter. When you cannot feel God tonight, remember that something is still happening underground. You will not fear the terror of the night. That does not mean the night has no terror. It means the terror does not get the last word. Endure. Call it what it is. Spring comes.

Psalm 118:6

[LXX 117]

With the LORD on my side I do not fear. What can mortals do to me?

❀ ❀ ❀

This psalm was sung at the entry into Jerusalem and at the Passover table, so Christ sang it just before Gethsemane. With the LORD on my side translates a Hebrew phrase that simply says the LORD is for me. Not general kindness. Taking sides. Felicity was a slave, imprisoned in Carthage with Perpetua and pregnant, and she gave birth in the cell two days before they were killed together. She cried out in labour, and a guard sneered that if she screamed at this, what would she do with the wild animals. She answered that now she suffers what she suffers, but then there will be Another in her suffering for her. She had just given birth, and she had the clearest theology in the building.

At the end Perpetua and Felicity went into the arena together. The account says that when the crowd saw them, one so young and one who had just given birth, even the crowd shuddered. The two women stood side by side and gave each other the kiss of peace [A]. They faced the worst holding each other up. The LORD was for them, and they were for each other. Ask God for a sister in faith tonight, and be one. With the LORD on my side I do not fear. What can anyone do to me?

Psalm 124:7

[LXX 123]

We have escaped like a bird from the snare of the fowlers; the snare is broken, and we have escaped.

❀ ❀ ❀

The whole verse is past tense. The bird is already out. Israel sang this walking up to the Temple, remembering. So say it as memory, not as a request. Thecla was a young woman of Iconium who broke off her engagement after hearing Paul preach. The second-century Acts of Paul and Thecla say she was sentenced to be burned and the fire did not take her, and thrown to wild beasts and they did not take her. Her story was copied and read aloud across Syria for centuries, mostly by women, who wanted a record of someone the trap did not hold. And look how the escape happened here. The trap broke. A bird cannot do that. You do not have to be strong enough to break it.

Thecla’s own mother turned against her, and the town called for her death [A]. She had almost no support from the people who should have loved her most. And still the trap broke, again and again. Some women walk the path of faith without their families understanding. If that is true for you, Thecla is your companion; the Church honours her as equal to the apostles. Say this psalm as memory tonight, even if you have to borrow her memory for now. The snare is broken, and we have escaped. Our help is in the name of the LORD.

Psalm 138:7

[LXX 137]

Though I walk in the midst of trouble, you preserve me against the wrath of my enemies; you stretch out your hand, and your right hand delivers me.

❀ ❀ ❀

Not out of trouble. In the midst of it, and the walking continues. This is not rescue by removal. In Mark 14 a woman walks into a room where she is not welcome, carrying perfume worth about a year’s wages, and breaks it over Him in front of men who say out loud that she is wasteful and that He should know what kind of woman she is. He lets them finish. Then He says: leave her alone, she has done a beautiful thing, and wherever the gospel is preached this will be told in memory of her. Two hands appear in this psalm, one reaching and one saving. He does not reach with one and work with the other. The reaching is the work.

She did not speak a single word in that room. Everything she meant was said in what she did. And Jesus defended her when everyone else attacked. Sometimes you will walk into places where you are not welcome, or where your faith is misunderstood. You do not always have to defend yourself. He may do it for you. Though I walk in the midst of trouble, you preserve me. The scent of what she poured out filled the house, and the whole world still remembers her. Nothing you pour out for Him is wasted.

Psalm 140:1

[LXX 139]

Deliver me, O LORD, from evildoers; protect me from those who are violent.

❀ ❀ ❀

This is the plainest kind of prayer there is. A verb and an object. No praise, no build-up, no theology. And Scripture keeps it. In Matthew 15 the Canaanite mother, after the silence and the refusals, is reduced to three words: Lord, help me. Matthew writes them down as a complete prayer that needs no improvement. There are nights when the only honest thing you can say is get me out. The Holy Spirit has already written that down for you, in the Psalms, so that you would never have to be ashamed of praying it. Being well spoken is not a condition of being heard. If it were, nobody would be heard at four in the morning.

Three words are enough when you have nothing else. Lord, help me. You can say it lying down, half asleep, crying or trembling. You can say it again a minute later. The Canaanite mother said it, and Jesus called her faith great [A]. He did not praise her eloquence. He praised her refusal to stop asking. Keep those three words close tonight, the way you might keep a glass of water beside your bed. If you wake suddenly, reach for them first. Deliver me, o LORD. Lord, help me.

Psalm 141:3–4

[LXX 140]

Set a guard over my mouth, O LORD; keep watch over the door of my lips. Do not turn my heart to any evil, to busy myself with wicked deeds.

❀ ❀ ❀

The psalm posts guards at two gates, the mouth and the heart, and the second is the real one. Do not turn my heart. He does not trust himself, and he says so to God rather than to nobody. The honesty is the guard. Much of Amma Syncletica’s teaching is about what to keep hidden: she compared a treasure put on display, quickly spent, to a virtue that is talked about [A]. There is a lot of pressure on women to explain themselves all the time, to account for every mood out loud. Some of what is happening in you right now is meant for God and one trusted person and nobody else. That is not secrecy. That is a guarded gate.

Syncletica also taught that there is a sorrow that helps and a sorrow that destroys, and that we must learn to tell them apart [A]. Sorrow that leads you towards God is good. Do not turn my heart to any evil, and that includes the evil of hating yourself. Speak to yourself tonight as gently as God speaks to you.

Psalm 144:1

[LXX 143]

Blessed be the LORD, my rock, who trains my hands for war, and my fingers for battle.

❀ ❀ ❀

Trains. The word assumes you did not know how, and assumes practice. Nobody is born able to fight at night. The book of Proverbs closes with a portrait of a woman, and the Hebrew calls her a woman of strength, using the same word used of an army. She is not described as delicate. She rolls up her sleeves and makes her arms strong. Strength and dignity are her clothing, and she laughs at the days to come. She laughs at the future, which is the thing that frightens everybody. That laugh is not a personality type. It is the result of training, the same way a strong arm is. This is why there are a hundred verses here and not one. The repeating is the training.

The woman of Proverbs is not perfect or famous. She is busy, practical, generous and strong, and the passage says her worth comes from fearing the LORD, not from charm or beauty [A]. That is good news for tired women. You do not have to be impressive to be strong. Strength is built from small, repeated things: getting up again, praying the same verse again, being kind again. Blessed be the LORD my rock, who trains my hands for war. Every repetition is training, and you are growing stronger even when it feels like nothing.

Wisdom 2:24

But through the devil’s envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his company experience it.

❀ ❀ ❀

Envy is the engine named here. Not hatred. Not strength. Envy, which is always an admission of lack. Ephrem imagines the moment exactly: the enemy sees a creature made of dust being crowned with a glory he has lost, and cannot bear to look at it [A]. That envy was first acted on in a garden, in Genesis 3, against a woman, because of what she had been given. Whatever moves against you in the dark does so because it sees in you something it does not have and cannot get. The Fathers all agreed what that is: the image of God, which in you is not damaged by a bad night and not reduced by fear. Nobody envies a nothing.

Envy always tries to make you feel worthless, because it cannot bear your worth. If dark thoughts tell you that you are nothing, that is the voice of envy, not of God. Ephrem sings that Adam and Eve were clothed in a robe of glory in Paradise, and that Christ clothes us in that glory again at baptism [A]. You are wearing a robe the enemy lost and can never get back. That is why he is jealous. Tonight, if you feel small and worthless, remember what you are wearing. The image of God in you is untouched.

Wisdom 3:1

But the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God, and no torment will ever touch them.

❀ ❀ ❀

This was written for people who had watched good people die badly, and the most famous of them is a mother. In 2 Maccabees 7 Shmuni, called Mart Shmuni in the Syriac tradition and remembered in your own calendar, watches seven sons killed one after another in a single day. She encourages each one in her own mother tongue. She says she does not know how they came into being in her womb, and that the Creator who formed them will give them back. She dies last. No torment will ever touch them was never a denial of what bystanders could see. It was a claim about where the soul actually was while the body was being handled.

Shmuni spoke to her sons in the language of their ancestors, so that the king could not follow what she said [A]. There is great comfort in that. In her worst hour she used words that belonged to her family and her faith. You have a language like that too: the prayers of your childhood, the hymns you know by heart in Syriac or in your own mother tongue, the words your mother or grandmother prayed. Use them tonight. The enemy has no share in them. And the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God.

Wisdom 7:30

For against wisdom evil does not prevail.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Bible’s longest picture of God’s own wisdom is a woman. Hokhmāh in Hebrew, Sophia in Greek, feminine in both. In Proverbs 8 and 9 she builds a house, sets a table, calls out from the city gates and walks the streets looking for the simple. In this book she is called a breath of the power of God and a spotless mirror. The verses just before this one compare her to light, and then comes this line. Night follows light, because that is how days work. But evil does not follow Wisdom, because it cannot keep up with her. John’s Gospel opens on the same logic. Darkness is on tonight’s schedule. Overcoming is not on the schedule at all.

In Proverbs Wisdom says: whoever finds me finds life [A]. She is not distant or cold. She stands at the crossroads calling to ordinary people. The early Church saw Christ Himself in this figure, and the great church of Constantinople was named Holy Wisdom. So when you pray for wisdom tonight, you are not asking for cleverness. You are asking for Christ, who meets you at your crossroads. Evil cannot keep up with Him. It does not matter how fast your fears run. Wisdom is faster.

Wisdom 10:12

She protected him from his enemies, and kept him safe from those who lay in wait for him; in his arduous contest she gave him the victory, so that he might learn that godliness is more powerful than anything else.

❀ ❀ ❀

This is about Jacob wrestling at the river, and Wisdom does not take him out of the fight. She gives him the victory inside it, and the text says why: so that he might learn. The struggle was not an interruption of his training. It was the training. His wife knew the same word. In Genesis 30 Rachel, after years of humiliation over having no children, names a son Naphtali, from the Hebrew word for wrestling, and says: with the wrestlings of God I have wrestled, and I have won. She used wrestling language about her own inner life decades before her husband met the angel at the ford. The women in this book were wrestling before the men were.

Rachel’s story has no neat ending. She finally had children of her own, and died giving birth to the second, calling him son of my sorrow [A]. Her life held real wrestling and real loss. But Jacob named the child Benjamin, son of my right hand, and her tomb became a place of prayer for centuries. Struggle and blessing are tangled together in her life, as they often are in ours. You do not have to win every match tonight. Keep holding on. Wisdom gives the victory inside the struggle, not only after it.

Sirach 2:1

My child, when you come to serve the Lord, prepare yourself for testing.

❀ ❀ ❀

This is startling honesty for a verse addressed to beginners. Serving God is not offered as the alternative to trouble. It is named as the occasion for it. Olympias was a wealthy young widow of Constantinople who refused to remarry, became a deaconess, and was exiled after John Chrysostom was. He wrote her seventeen letters from his own exile, and nearly all of them are about this. She was sinking under heaviness of spirit, and he kept telling her the storm was not proof she had taken a wrong turn [A]. He never once told her she should not be struggling. If you have been wondering whether these nights mean you have done something wrong, Sirach answers before you finish asking. They may mean you have started.

Chrysostom told Olympias that despondency was a heavier burden than any outward trouble, and that she should resist it as she would resist any other enemy [A]. He took her inner suffering seriously. He never told her she was exaggerating. If a heaviness has settled on you, it is real, and it deserves care. Rest when you can. Eat. Go out into the daylight. Talk to your confessor or a trusted friend, and see a doctor if it does not lift. Prepare yourself for testing does not mean bear everything alone.

Sirach 2:4–5

Accept whatever befalls you, and in times of humiliation be patient. For gold is tested in the fire, and those found acceptable, in the furnace of humiliation.

❀ ❀ ❀

Gold is tested in fire. People are tested in humiliation. Notice that the second furnace is social and inward, not physical. There is an obvious example, and the Church has never looked away from it. A young woman, unmarried and visibly pregnant in a village where everybody knew everybody. In Matthew 1 her fiancé decides to divorce her quietly, which was the merciful option available to him, before the angel comes to him in a dream. She was carrying God, and to the whole street it looked like disgrace. The furnace was entirely one of reputation. Isaac of Nineveh says humility is the clothing God put on to come among us [A]. If that is true, the furnace is a fitting room.

Mary never defended herself to the village. We do not hear of her explaining or arguing. God sent an angel to Joseph, and later He honoured her before the whole world, so that, as she sang, all generations now call her blessed [A]. The humiliation you suffer is not the end of your story. If you feel ashamed of these nights, or ashamed of being afraid, keep Mary close. God knows the truth about you. In time He will show it. Gold comes out of the furnace shining.

Sirach 2:6

Trust in him, and he will help you; make your ways straight, and hope in him.

❀ ❀ ❀

Three instructions, and the middle one is yours to do. Trust, straighten, hope. He will help, but something is asked, and what is asked is ordinary daytime work. Straightening a path means what you watch, what you forgive, when you eat, when you put the phone down, whether you tell anyone the truth about how you are. Martha’s work in Luke 10 is never called wrong; what Jesus addresses is the worry around it, and it is Martha who makes the great confession of faith in John 11. Keep the work. Put down the frantic part of it. Night fears are very often fed in the afternoon, by things that looked at the time like being responsible.

It is easy to think of faith as only what happens in church. But Sirach says straighten your ways, and Martha shows that ordinary work can be holy too: cooking, cleaning, studying, working, caring for others. All of it can be done with God. Tonight, think gently about tomorrow afternoon. What is one small thing you could change that might help your nights? Less news or scrolling before bed, a short walk, a prayer before sleep. Small straight paths make a large difference.

Sirach 4:28

Fight to the death for truth, and the Lord God will fight for you.

❀ ❀ ❀

Fight for truth. In a season of fear that means refusing to let fear tell you what is real. The lie is almost never about whether God exists. It is about how God feels towards you, and it is always the same lie, because he has only ever had one. Catherine of Alexandria is remembered in the early martyr accounts as a young woman of learning who was set against a panel of philosophers hired by the emperor to argue her out of her faith. She took their arguments apart one at a time, and several of them changed sides. A girl, outnumbered, on the arguments. And the verse ends with a reversal: you fight, and God turns out to be the one fighting.

Catherine was young, educated and confident, and the emperor tried to make her doubt everything she knew. Many women are told, in large and small ways, that their minds cannot be trusted. Fear at night does the same thing. It says: you are imagining it, you are foolish, you cannot think clearly. You do not have to win every argument tonight. You only have to hold on to one truth, that God loves you and is fighting for you. Fight to the death for truth, and the Lord God will fight for you.

Sirach 21:2

Flee from sin as from a snake; for if you approach sin, it will bite you. Its teeth are lion’s teeth, and can destroy human lives.

❀ ❀ ❀

The advice here is to run, not to fight. It is one of the very few places in Scripture where running is the recommendation. And notice what makes the snake dangerous: being near it. Most of what we call temptation is a failure of distance, not a failure of strength. Amma Sarah, the desert mother whose sayings monks collected, lived sixty years in a cell overlooking a river and never once lifted her eyes to look at it [A]. Not because rivers are evil. Because she had decided once what she would look at and did not reopen the question every evening. Decide in daylight what you will not do at night. Which screen, which room, which conversation, which memory. Then you will not have to win that argument at two in the morning.

Distance is not cowardice. It is wisdom. Sarah chose not to look at the river because she knew what she was protecting. You may need some distance right now from certain stories, certain people, or certain habits that feed your fear, and keeping that distance is not weakness. It is guarding your heart. Make the decision once, in daylight, and keep it. Flee from sin as from a snake. Then, when the night comes, you will not have to argue with yourself. You will already have decided.

Sirach 23:1

O Lord, Father and Master of my life, do not abandon me to their counsel, and do not let me fall because of them!

❀ ❀ ❀

The prayer is not to be spared enemies. It is not to be handed over to their advice. That is the real danger in a frightening dream. Not the picture, but what you are told it means afterwards. Luke 24 records what happened when the first women told the truth about what they had seen at the tomb. Their words seemed to the men like an idle tale, and they did not believe them. The Greek word is lēros, a medical term for the rambling speech of someone delirious. The men who loved them most reached for a word for hysteria. Do not be handed over to that, from others or from inside your own head. You are not raving.

Peter, at least, got up and ran to the tomb to see for himself [A]. The women’s words, dismissed as nonsense, were what sent him running. The truth they carried could not be stopped by being disbelieved. If people do not take your fear seriously, or tell you it is all in your head, that hurts. But your words still matter, and God still hears them. Tell the truth about how you are to someone who will listen. And pray Sirach’s prayer tonight: Father and Master of my life, do not abandon me to their counsel.

Judith 9:11

For your strength does not depend on numbers, nor your might on the powerful. But you are the God of the lowly, helper of the oppressed, upholder of the weak, protector of the forsaken, savior of those without hope.

❀ ❀ ❀

Five titles, and every one of them is about position: lowly, oppressed, weak, forsaken, without hope. Judith was a wealthy widow of Bethulia who had kept three years of mourning and was known mainly for being devout. She prayed this in sackcloth at the hour of evening incense, while the elders of her town were preparing to surrender. Then she washed, dressed, and walked out of a besieged city into the enemy camp with nothing but her wits and one maid. Nobody expected it of her, which is exactly why it worked. Pray this verse by finding yourself in its list. You will find yourself, because the list was written for people with no other resources.

Judith did not wait to feel brave before she acted. She prayed first, and then dressed carefully, as if for a celebration, and went [A]. There is something holy about getting ready even when you are afraid. Washing your face, combing your hair, putting on clean clothes: these can be acts of faith. Tonight, if the fear is heavy, do one small thing to care for yourself before you lie down. Then pray her prayer: you are the God of the lowly, helper of the oppressed, protector of the forsaken. You are my God.

Baruch 4:21

Take courage, my children, cry to God, and he will deliver you from the power and hand of the enemy.

❀ ❀ ❀

Take courage, my children. The one speaking is a mother. Through the whole of Baruch 4 the city of Jerusalem speaks in a woman’s voice, as a widow calling after the children who have been taken from her. She says she will not keep quiet, that she watched them go, that she cannot help them and will not stop calling. It is written to exiles with no visible hope of return, and the only instruction is cry to God, given to people who have already lost. Courage is commanded here in a woman’s voice, to people she cannot reach. Permission to cry out is itself a kind of strength, and it is the kind most often withheld from the woman expected to be the steady one.

A little later in the same passage Jerusalem says: I sent you out with sorrow and weeping, but God will give you back to me with joy and gladness for ever [A]. The mother who cries out is also the mother who is comforted. Your crying out now is not the end; it is the beginning of being heard. Many women hold their tears in because others need them to be strong. Tonight you may let them out to God if you need to. Take courage. Cry to God. He delivers from the hand of the enemy.

1 Maccabees 3:19

It is not on the size of the army that victory in battle depends, but strength comes from Heaven.

❀ ❀ ❀

Judas says this to men who can count and who know the numbers are bad. He does not argue with the arithmetic. And in Mark 12 Jesus sits down opposite the temple treasury, watching the money go in, and says the quiet thing out loud. A widow has put in two coins, the smallest currency in circulation, and she has given more than all of them, because they gave out of their spare and she gave everything she had to live on. He overturned the whole ledger from a bench, in public, in favour of a woman nobody had counted. Strength comes from Heaven is not a denial of the odds. It says the odds are not the whole list of relevant facts.

The widow did not know Jesus was watching. She did not know her two coins would be talked about for two thousand years. She simply gave what she had. Your small acts of faith tonight are being watched the same way, with love: one verse said in the dark, one prayer when you are too tired, one sign of the cross. They look tiny. Heaven counts them differently. It is not on the size of the army that victory depends. Strength comes from Heaven, and Heaven notices the widow first.

2 Maccabees 8:18

For they trust to arms and acts of daring, but we trust in the Almighty God, who is able with a single nod to strike down those who are coming against us, and even, if necessary, the whole world.

❀ ❀ ❀

A single nod. The Greek word means the smallest gesture a person can make. Not a battle. Not an effort. A nod. This is the difference the Fathers insist on: between the Creator and anything created there is no contest, only a decision [C]. And the decision that undid the enemy’s whole position was also one short sentence, spoken by a girl in a village with no witnesses: let it be to me according to your word. The Fathers treat her consent as the hinge of the entire rescue. Whatever is gathering against you costs God no effort at all to undo. And your own small yes tonight is not small either.

Mary’s yes did not make her life easy. It led to Bethlehem, to Egypt, to Nazareth and to the Cross. It also led to the Resurrection, to Pentecost, and to her place in every prayer of the Church. Saying yes to God does not mean your nights will be free of fear. It means you will not face them without Him. Tonight you can say your own small yes: let it be to me according to your word, Lord, whatever tonight brings. That one sentence is enough. God can undo everything against you with a single nod.

Matthew 6:13

And do not bring us to the time of trial, but rescue us from the evil one.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Lord’s Prayer ends on the night’s request, and the Greek is best read as a person rather than a thing. This is a prayer about someone. You have been saying the last line in Syriac since you were small, the way the Peshitta has it, before you had any theology to put with it, and most people learn it at home from the women of the family long before anyone explains it. That was deliberate. Christ built this request into the prayer He expected ordinary people to say every day of their lives. That is the thing to notice tonight. He did not think your fear was too much, or your asking strange. He assumed it would come up most nights, so He wrote you a line for it.

Notice that the prayer is plural. Deliver us from the evil one. You never pray it alone. Every time you say it, the whole Church is saying it with you, in every language, at every hour of the day and night somewhere in the world. If you learned it kneeling beside a mother or grandmother, she is still praying it with you now. Tonight, say it slowly, and when you reach the last line, say it twice. Rescue us from the evil one. For yours is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory.

Matthew 26:41

Stay awake and pray that you may not come into the time of trial; the spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.

❀ ❀ ❀

He said this in Gethsemane to friends who could not stay awake, and He does not scold them. The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak. That is a diagnosis said kindly. If you fall asleep in the middle of praying tonight, you are in the garden with the apostles, and He knows what you are made of because He made it. But hold the rest of that week beside it. The men slept, and then ran. According to Mark 15 and Luke 23 the women watched the crucifixion, noted where the body was laid, went home, prepared spices, and kept the Sabbath before returning. In the Gospels, staying awake is not about being strong. It is about not leaving.

Staying is one of the strongest things a woman can do. The women at the Cross could not stop what was happening. They could not even get close; Mark says they watched from a distance. But they stayed. Tonight, if you cannot pray well, if you fall asleep halfway through, if you feel weak, do not be troubled. The flesh is weak, Jesus said, and He was not angry when He said it. Only do not leave. Turn back to Him each time you wake. That is what watching means. The women who stayed were the first to see Him alive.

Luke 10:18

I watched Satan fall from heaven like a flash of lightning.

❀ ❀ ❀

Lightning is fast and bright and already over by the time you notice it. Christ reports the fall as something He watched. Past. Seen. Finished. And Luke, who records this, is the one who tells us in chapter 8 that Mary Magdalene was a woman out of whom seven demons had gone. Whatever that meant, it means she had been at the far edge of what the enemy can do to a person. And in John 20 she is the first person the risen Christ speaks to, and the one sent to tell the apostles. The woman with the worst history in the Gospel gets the news first. Whatever appears in your dreams acting like a ruler is a sacked official still wearing the uniform.

Mary Magdalene knew more about the enemy’s power than most people ever will, and she was the one trusted with the greatest news in history. Her past did not disqualify her. It made her a witness. If the enemy has been loud in your life, that does not mean you are far from God. It may mean you will one day understand His victory better than others do. Christ watched Satan fall like lightning; Mary saw the risen Lord in a garden. Some day your story too may comfort a woman who is where you are now.

Luke 22:31–32

Simon, Simon, listen! Satan has demanded to sift all of you like wheat, but I have prayed for you that your own faith may not fail; and you, when once you have turned back, strengthen your brothers.

❀ ❀ ❀

Satan has to ask. He has no right of access to you on his own. And then: I have prayed for you. Jesus does not pray that Peter be spared the sifting, but that his faith will not fail inside it, and He speaks of the recovery as already settled. When you have turned back. At the wedding in Cana, in John 2, His mother noticed the wine had run out before anyone else did, said four words about it, and told the servants to do whatever He said. Those are her last recorded words in Scripture. Someone always notices on your behalf before you can put it into words. He is praying for you in the same tense He used about Peter.

At Cana Jesus first said that His hour had not yet come. Mary did not argue. She simply told the servants: do whatever He tells you [A]. She trusted Him to act in His own time, and He did, more generously than anyone expected. Sometimes the answer to your nights may seem slow. Like Mary, you can bring the problem to Him and then wait with trust. Jesus prayed for Peter before Peter knew he would fall. He is praying for you before you know what tomorrow holds. And when you have turned back, you will strengthen your sisters.

John 8:44

He was a murderer from the beginning and does not stand in the truth, because there is no truth in him. When he lies, he speaks according to his own nature, for he is a liar and the father of lies.

❀ ❀ ❀

Jesus names the enemy’s two trades, murder and lying, and the second serves the first. He does not stand in the truth. There is no ground under him at all. And the first lie in the Bible was told to a woman in a garden, and it was not about whether God exists. It was about His intentions: did He really say that, you will not really die, He is holding something back from you. Every lie since has been a version of that one. He has not had a new idea in all of history. Ask of every night thought: who benefits if I believe this about God?

Eve’s mistake was not that she was weak. It was that she listened to a voice that questioned God’s goodness, and then kept talking with it. That is why the Fathers say: do not hold conversations with the enemy. If a thought tonight says God does not care, God is far away, God is punishing you, recognise the old lie. It is the same one from the garden. Answer it with Scripture, as Christ did: the LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble. Then stop listening. The father of lies has nothing new to say.

John 12:31

Now is the judgment of this world; now the ruler of this world will be driven out.

❀ ❀ ❀

He said this days before the Cross, as the verdict on it. Now. The judging of the world happens at the exact moment the world condemns Him. And look what John puts just before it in the same chapter. Six days before the Passover, Mary of Bethany takes a pound of pure nard, pours it on His feet, wipes them with her hair, and the whole house fills with the smell while a man complains about the cost. The week in which the ruler of this world is cast out opens with a woman pouring out everything she had on the floor. He is now trespassing in a house that has changed hands.

When the man complained about the cost, Jesus said: leave her alone; she bought it so that she might keep it for the day of my burial [A]. Mary understood what was coming before the disciples did. Her love made her see clearly. Women often notice what others miss. Trust your sense of God’s nearness. Pour out your love to Him tonight, even if it feels wasteful, even if others think your prayers are too much. The ruler of this world is being driven out, and the house is already filling with the fragrance of what you give.

John 14:30

The ruler of this world is coming. He has no power over me.

❀ ❀ ❀

The Greek is closer to he has nothing in me. No foothold. No handle. Nothing of his own to grab. Maximus the Confessor reads the Incarnation this way: Christ took our whole human nature, but without the handle that sin had given the enemy on it [A]. And He took that nature from one place only. From the body of a woman, with her agreement, in the ordinary way. There was no other source. That is why the Church has never been able to speak of Mary as an extra. The humanity that gives the enemy nothing to hold was knit together in her. Baptism is where that same nature is handed on to you. You are wearing it now, including while you sleep.

The Church calls Mary the Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God, and your own Church remembers her in its prayers again and again, because she is the place where God came close. Through her, our human nature became His own. That means your body, your mind, your fears and your hopes are all things He has taken on and made holy. The enemy finds no handle in Christ, and he will not find one in you while you stay in Him. Tonight, rest in the body God gave you. He knows it from the inside.

Romans 8:31

What then are we to say about these things? If God is for us, who is against us?

❀ ❀ ❀

Paul asks a question and refuses to answer it, because the answer is obviously nothing. For us translates a Greek word that means standing in someone else’s place. He is not just sympathetic. He has taken your side by taking your position. And think how this sentence reached the world. In Romans 16 Paul introduces Phoebe, a deacon of the church at Cenchreae and a patron of many people including himself, and asks the Romans to give her whatever she needs. That is the standard way of describing someone carrying a letter. If God is for us travelled to Rome in a woman’s bag. She would have read it out loud and answered the first questions about it.

Phoebe made a long journey across the sea to carry this letter, protecting the pages from weather and thieves. She was trusted with words that would shape the whole Church. You are carrying something precious too: the faith given to you, the verses you are learning, the prayers you say at night. Carry them carefully. And when you feel that everything is against you, unfold Phoebe’s letter in your mind and read the question again, slowly, as she may once have read it aloud. If God is for us, who is against us?

Romans 8:37–39

No, in all these things we are more than conquerors through him who loved us. For I am convinced that neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor rulers, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord.

❀ ❀ ❀

Paul lists everything he can think of: death, life, angels, rulers, powers, height, depth. The list is exhausting on purpose. He wants you to find your own category in it and then find it crossed out. And remember who was in the room when this was read. Tryphaena and Tryphosa, Persis, Julia, Nereus’ sister, all greeted a few verses later. Names of that kind in Rome usually belonged to slaves and freedwomen. For them, rulers and powers were not an idea. They were a household, an owner, a legal status. Paul tells that room none of it can separate them from the love of God. The promise is not that these things stop existing. It is that they cannot reach that far.

Those women in Rome may have had very little control over their own lives. They could be bought, sold or punished by others. And Paul told them that nothing, absolutely nothing, could separate them from the love of God. If you feel powerless tonight, that promise is yours too. Make your own list and say it aloud: not fear, not dreams, not memories, not this night, not anything I have done, not anything done to me, will be able to separate me from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord. Then say amen.

1 Corinthians 10:13

No testing has overtaken you that is not common to everyone. God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your strength, but with the testing he will also provide the way out so that you may be able to endure it.

❀ ❀ ❀

The first mercy here is simply that you are not unusual. Fear tells you nobody else has had this, that something is wrong with you specifically. The verse says common. The women of the Church walked this and left written records on purpose, so that nobody coming after them would think she was the first: Syncletica, Theodora and Sarah in the Egyptian desert, Olympias in Constantinople, Macrina in Cappadocia, Mary of Egypt beyond the Jordan. Then two promises arrive in one sentence, a limit and a way out. The Greek word for way out means an exit, the mouth of a mountain pass. There is a way through. It is not the way back and it is not the way round, and you usually only see it from inside.

Each of those women had a different struggle. Syncletica had illness. Olympias had exile and despondency. Macrina had grief. Mary of Egypt had her memories. Sarah had temptation. Theodora had her winter. None of their struggles was exactly like yours, and yet all of them were common in the sense this verse means. They are part of the great cloud of witnesses around you [A]. Tonight choose one of them as your companion and ask her to pray for you. Then trust the promise: God is faithful, and He will provide the way out.

2 Corinthians 10:4–5

For the weapons of our warfare are not merely human, but they have divine power to destroy strongholds. We destroy arguments and every proud obstacle raised up against the knowledge of God, and we take every thought captive to obey Christ.

❀ ❀ ❀

Strongholds, arguments, thoughts. Paul’s whole battlefield is inside you. And take captive is a slow verb. It does not say destroy every thought. It says take them prisoner and bring them to Christ. Macrina does this in front of us. In On the Soul and the Resurrection Gregory arrives at her deathbed with his grief in pieces, and she takes his objections one at a time and answers each rather than telling him not to raise them: that the soul dissolves, that the body is lost, that love ends. She walked each of them to the resurrection. She led them. She did not kill them. You do not have to win the argument with a frightening thought. You only have to march it into His presence and leave it there.

You can treat your own thoughts the same way. When a frightening thought comes tonight, do not fight it angrily. Take it gently to Christ: Lord, here is this fear; I give it to you. Then let it go, the way you might hand a crying child to someone who can comfort it better. Every thought taken captive. Every thought held.

2 Corinthians 12:9

My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness.

❀ ❀ ❀

Paul asked three times for the thing to be taken away and was refused. That should be said plainly, not softened. The answer was not the end of the thorn but enough grace inside it. Hannah had worked this out a thousand years earlier and put it in a song in 1 Samuel 2: the bows of the mighty are broken, but the weak are given strength; the woman who had no children has borne seven, and the one with many is left alone. Mary sang the same thing in her own words in Luke 1. Twice in Scripture, a thousand years apart, this theology comes to us as a woman’s hymn. Your weakness is not the obstacle to His power. It is the place it works.

Hannah sang her song after giving her son to God. Mary sang hers before her son was even born. One sang after a costly gift, the other before a costly future, and both sang about God lifting up the weak. You may be weak tonight. That is not a problem for God. My grace is sufficient for you, He told Paul, for power is made perfect in weakness. Let your weakness be the place where you sing tonight. Even one quiet line is a song, and power is being made perfect in you while you sing it.

Ephesians 6:11–12

For our struggle is not against enemies of blood and flesh, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers of this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places.

❀ ❀ ❀

Every piece of the armour is His, not yours. You put on what already exists. And verse 12 changes the whole picture: you are not fighting anyone you can see, which also means nobody you can see is your enemy. Getting the opponent right is already half the armour. Scripture has a matching picture for a woman at the end of Proverbs 31: Strength and dignity are her clothing. Clothing, not character. Something put on, daily, on purpose, from outside, by a woman who got dressed that morning whether or not she felt like it. You do not have to produce the armour out of your own supplies. You reach for it, and it is already there, and it fits.

Colossians 1:13

He has rescued us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of his beloved Son.

❀ ❀ ❀

Two verbs, both past tense. Rescued. Transferred. The Greek word was used for moving whole populations from one kingdom to another. A change of citizenship, not a change of mood. Ruth is the clearest case in Scripture. Moabites were excluded from the assembly of Israel by law, and she was a widow from Moab who said where you go I will go and your God will be my God, and crossed over with nothing. Matthew names her in the fourth verse of the New Testament, in the family tree of Christ. She did not feel Israelite. She was transferred. The darkness may still have your old address and may still write to it. It has no authority over you there any more.

Ruth chose to belong to God’s people, and God made sure she belonged. She became the great-grandmother of King David, and through him an ancestor of Christ [A]. A woman from outside was placed at the very centre of God’s story. If you sometimes feel like an outsider, in your family, your church, or even your own life, Ruth’s story is yours. You have been transferred. You belong to the kingdom of the beloved Son, and darkness has no claim on you. Where He goes, you go. His people are your people.

Colossians 2:15

He disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public example of them, triumphing over them in it.

The picture is a Roman victory parade, with the defeated enemy marched disarmed through the streets. And Christ did this in it. In the Cross. In the moment that looked most like total defeat. Whatever humiliates you tonight is not automatically proof of loss. You are not a reliable judge of what a defeat looks like from the inside. Neither was anyone else on that hill.

The women watched from a distance because coming closer was dangerous. They were not brave in a dramatic way. They were faithful in a quiet way, and that kind of faithfulness counts. Your quiet prayers tonight, said from a distance, half believing, still count. He disarmed the rulers and authorities and made a public example of them. The enemy that troubles your dreams has already been led through the streets in defeat. You are standing on the right side of that hill, even if it does not feel like it yet.

2 Timothy 1:7

For God did not give us a spirit of cowardice, but rather a spirit of power and of love and of self-discipline.

❀ ❀ ❀

Lois and Eunice probably never knew how far their faith would travel. They taught a boy at home, by example and from the Scriptures, and that boy became Paul’s beloved companion and a leader of the Church. Paul reminds him that he has known the sacred writings since childhood [A]. The faith of mothers and grandmothers shapes the world. Perhaps someone in your family passed the faith on to you. Thank God for her tonight. And know that the courage she had lives in you too. God did not give you a spirit of fear.

Hebrews 2:14–15

So that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and free those who all their lives were held in slavery by the fear of death.

❀ ❀ ❀

The text names the real chain. Not death, but the lifelong fear of it. That fear is what being enslaved to the enemy actually consists of, and it is what every terrifying dream is built out of. Now hold this tonight. Preparing a body for burial was women’s work in that world, as it still is in much of yours: washing it, wrapping it, sitting with it, keeping the night watch beside it. So when the Gospel needs someone to walk towards a corpse in the dark with spices in her hands, it does not have to invent anybody. The myrrhbearers went towards death because that was their ordinary job. And they found it broken, from the inside, by Someone who had gone in first.

In many places women still keep vigil beside the dead and sing laments over them. The myrrhbearers belong to that long line. They did not avoid death. They met it with love and with spices, and because they faced it, they were the first to find it defeated. Christ came to free those who all their lives were held in slavery by the fear of death. That includes you. Tonight, if your fear goes all the way down to death itself, remember this: He went in first, and He came out. The tomb is empty.

1 Peter 5:8–9

Discipline yourselves, keep alert. Like a roaring lion your adversary the devil prowls around, looking for someone to devour. Resist him, steadfast in your faith.

❀ ❀ ❀

Like a roaring lion. Peter chooses a comparison, not an identification, and that difference is the whole verse. Athanasius records Antony saying the demons make noise precisely because noise is what they have left [A]. A roar is a sound, and sounds are made by creatures that have to announce themselves. The Acts of Paul and Thecla say that when Thecla was thrown to the beasts, a lioness came and lay down at her feet, and then fought the other animals to defend her, and died doing it. A real lion, behaving better than the one in the comparison. Resist, steadfast in your faith: standing still is the resisting. You are not asked to roar back.

The account says that the women in the crowd cried out against what was being done to Thecla, and when she was finally set free they shouted praise to God [A]. Other women saw her suffering and spoke up for her. You are not alone in your arena either. Your sisters in faith, living and departed, are standing with you. The devil prowls like a lion, but Christ is the Lion of Judah and He has already won. Stand firm tonight, knowing that your brothers and sisters all over the world are going through the same.

Revelation 12:11

But they have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they did not cling to life even in the face of death.

❀ ❀ ❀

Look at the chapter you are standing in. Revelation 12 opens with a woman clothed with the sun, crying out in labour, with a dragon waiting in front of her to eat the child the moment it is born. She is not eaten. She is given the two wings of a great eagle and carried into the wilderness, where she is fed for as long as it takes. The dragon is furious and goes off to make war on the rest of her children, which is you. That is the setting of this verse. Three things conquer: His blood, their testimony, and their willingness to lose. Not one of the three is strength. Now sleep. You are covered.

The Church has read this woman as Israel, as the Church, and as Mary, and all three readings are true. You belong to all three: you are part of God’s people, part of the Church, and a daughter of Mary. The dragon was real and furious, and still the woman was carried, protected and fed. God had prepared a place for her in the wilderness [A]. He has prepared a place for you too, even in this season of frightening nights. They conquered him by the blood of the Lamb. And the Lamb is yours.

Say it out loud. Then stop.

About

Why a Hundred

Around the year 390, in a desert settlement in Egypt, a monk named Evagrius put together a book called the Antirrhētikos, which means “Talking Back.” It is almost nothing but Bible verses, hundreds of them, sorted by the particular thought each one answers [A]. His idea is the idea behind these pages. The enemy does not arrive announcing himself. He arrives as a thought. And a thought is not answered by arguing, which only keeps the conversation going. It is answered by a word that is not your own.

So this is not a book to read through. These hundred are meant to be used. Learn a few by heart. Say them out loud. Repeat them until they are in your mouth before they reach your mind. Christ fought this way. In the desert He answered three temptations with three quotations and nothing else. No reasoning with the tempter. No explaining Himself [A].

Written for You

Every reflection in this book is built around a woman. That is not decoration. The tradition you belong to is full of women who were awake in the dark and left a record of it. You should not have to translate someone else’s experience into your own before it can help you.

Some of them are in the Bible. Hagar alone in a desert. Miriam standing still beside a river. Rizpah on a rock keeping animals off her sons from harvest until the rains came. Rahab inside a wall. Esther fasting three nights before a decision she did not feel ready for. Susanna accused by men whose word counted more than hers. Hannah misjudged by a priest. The woman who reached through a crowd for the edge of a garment. Martha, who said both halves of the sentence. And the women who walked towards a tomb before dawn, because the work of death has always been theirs.

Some come from the centuries after. Perpetua, who dreamt of a dragon at the foot of a ladder and wrote down that she used its head as the first step. Felicity, who gave birth in a prison cell. Thecla. Barbara in her tower. Macrina, teaching her brother out of his grief while she was the one dying. Syncletica, Sarah and Theodora in the Egyptian desert. Olympias, who received seventeen letters about heaviness of heart. Mary of Egypt. Mart Shmuni, whom your own Church sings. And the Daughters of the Covenant, who stood in choir in Edessa and Nisibis and sang Ephrem’s hymns through the night, so that the faith of the Syriac Church lived in women’s memories [A].

There is a short list of all of them at the end of this book, with a line about each one, in case you meet a name you do not know.

None of them was spared. Every one of them was kept.

A Word on Dreams

The Fathers say very little about dreams, and their silence is a kindness. John Climacus warns beginners that dreams deserve no attention at all. Diadochos of Photikē says plainly that they are not to be trusted, because a sleeping soul cannot tell who is speaking to it [A]. The reasoning is simple. The enemy has very little power over a sleeping woman. He has a great deal of power over a waking woman who spends the next day working out what the dream meant [C].

So the counsel is not understand this. It is do not stay in the conversation. Wake up, cross yourself, say a verse, and let the pictures go without examining them. They are not a message. They do not need decoding. The attention you refuse to give them is the weapon.

And do not let anyone else hand you their interpretation either. When the first women told the truth about what they had seen at the tomb, the men who loved them said it was an idle tale. The Greek word is lēros, which means the babbling of someone delirious [A]. Being disbelieved is its own kind of night. You are not raving. You are not fragile. You are not being punished.

The same Fathers say a bad night can come from three places: the state of the body, the weight the soul is carrying, and the enemy’s suggestion. They do not treat the first two as less spiritual [A]. So if there are many of these nights, be kind to your body as well. Rest, food, daylight, your confessor, and a doctor if one is needed. In the Orthodox view the body is not the lesser half of you, and looking after it is not the lesser remedy [C].

The Wakeful Ones

The Syriac tradition calls the angels ʿirē, which means the Wakeful, or the Watchers. The name comes from a vision in the book of Daniel [A]. It is worth keeping near you. While you sleep, someone assigned to you does not. Your sleep is not a gap in the guard. The Church divides the night office into watches for the same reason, so that no hour of the dark is without somebody awake in it. When you wake at three in the morning, you are not the only one awake. You have joined a service already going on [C].

And the picture the Church reached for, from Constantinople to Kerala, was not a wall or a sword. It was a woman spreading her veil over a frightened congregation at night. A veil is what you lay over a sleeping child. It works by being there, not by force. It is enough.

How to Use These

Choose five or six and learn them by heart this week. Not more. A verse you have to look up is a verse you will not have when you need it.

Keep one for the moment of waking, before anything else is thought or decided. Say it, then stop. Do not add an explanation. Do not argue. Do not check whether the verse fits the dream.

Read the reflections in daylight. Say the verses in the dark.

Say them out loud where you can. The body prays too, and a frightened body settles faster to a sound than to a thought.

Decide in daylight what you will not do at night. Which screen, which room, which conversation, which memory. Amma Sarah lived sixty years beside a river and never lifted her eyes to look at it, not because rivers are evil but because she had settled the question once and did not reopen it every evening [A].

Tell one person the truth about how you are. Not everyone. One.

When the hundred are finished, start again. Repeating them is not a lack of imagination. It is the training, and the training is already the victory, arriving in pieces too small to feel.

A Last Word

In a season like this one it can feel as though every night puts you back in the same place. The same fear, the same hour, nothing gained. Gregory of Nyssa’s answer to exactly this is a word he made famous, epektasis, which means always straining forward. The life of the soul with God is not a circle but a constant reaching ahead, where coming back round is never just repetition [A]. You are not going round and round. You are climbing a spiral staircase. The view looks the same at every turn because the window faces one way, not because you have not risen [C].

He learned to say it, by his own account, sitting beside his sister while she was dying and she was the one comforting him.
